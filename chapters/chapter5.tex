\chapter{Q Feed-Forward}
\label{ch:qff}

The motion model of the tracker, introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:tt_cca}, assumes that
the opponent moves with constant curvature and constant longitudinal acceleration over
each prediction interval. This assumption holds well when the dynamics of the opponent
changes slowly, e.g.\ on a straight or in the middle of a corner, but it is violated at
the beginning and at the end of the maneuvers. When the opponent starts braking, or
passes from braking to traction, the acceleration changes abruptly, and the actual
motion departs from the one predicted by the CCA model. In these conditions, the dynamic
model is no longer correct and the quality of the estimate degrades.

This behavior is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_cca_baseline}, which compares the speed and the
acceleration estimated by the filter with the CCA model and the nominal process noise with the
ground truth over two braking maneuvers. At the onset of each braking, the estimated
acceleration departs from the ground truth with a delay of several tenths of a second, and the
lag accumulated in this interval is reflected on the speed, which is overestimated during the
braking and underestimated at its end.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/ch5/cca_baseline.pdf}
    \caption{Speed and acceleration estimated with the CCA model and the nominal process
    noise. Session of 5 September 2026, Imola.}
    \label{fig:qff_cca_baseline}
\end{figure}

Among the approaches proposed in the literature to adapt a filter to the maneuvers of a target,
one of the most widespread is the multiple model approach. Its rationale is to describe the
possible dynamics of the opponent through a set of motion models, each of which is evolved by a
dedicated filter running in parallel, so that the estimate is obtained by combining the outputs
of the filters. In the classical multiple model formulation, the filters evolve independently
and their estimates are only combined at the output, weighted by model probabilities computed
from the likelihood of the innovations. In the Interacting Multiple Model (IMM) filter,
instead, the filters also interact, since at each step their initial conditions are obtained by
mixing the estimates of all the models according to the transition probabilities between them,
which allows the estimate to follow the switches between the operating conditions of the
vehicle. These approaches, however, come at the cost of a higher computational load and of a
larger number of parameters to tune, such as the transition probabilities between the models,
and they require the actual behavior of the vehicle to be effectively represented by the chosen
set of models.

A different approach is based on a maneuver detection logic, and it can be seen as a nuance of
the multiple model approach. The filter still switches between distinct behaviors, but these
are obtained from a single motion model by changing its process noise, and the switch is
driven by an explicit detection of the maneuver rather than by model probabilities computed
from the innovations. The approach presented in this chapter moves from this second family, and
it consists in adapting the process noise covariance matrix following the detection of the
maneuver, i.e.\ in feed-forward, hence its name.

Section~\ref{sec:qff_idea} explains the idea and the rationale of the approach, and
Section~\ref{sec:qff_nominal} describes the preliminary tuning of the nominal process noise,
which is the starting value of the method. Section~\ref{sec:qff_formulation} presents the
feed-forward formulation, together with the maneuver detection logic based on the jerk of the
opponent and its implementation in the tracker. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:qff_results}
compares the resulting estimate with the one of the nominal filter.

\section{The Idea}
\label{sec:qff_idea}

The idea behind the approach stems from the role of the process noise covariance
$\mathbf{Q}_k$ in the Kalman filter, where it represents the uncertainty of the motion model,
i.e.\ how much the actual motion of the opponent is expected to differ from the predicted one.
In the prediction step, it is added to the covariance propagated through the motion model,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{P}_{k+1|k} = \mathbf{F}_k\,\mathbf{P}_{k|k}\,\mathbf{F}_k^\top + \mathbf{Q}_k,
    \label{eq:qff_pred_cov}
\end{equation}
and the predicted covariance enters the Kalman gain of the correction step,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{K}_{k+1} = \mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}\,\mathbf{H}_{k+1}^\top
    \left(\mathbf{H}_{k+1}\,\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}\,\mathbf{H}_{k+1}^\top + \mathbf{R}_{k+1}\right)^{-1},
    \label{eq:qff_gain}
\end{equation}
as in~\eqref{eq:tt_prediction} and~\eqref{eq:tt_update}. Considering a single component,
with a predicted variance $P$ and a measurement noise variance $R$, the gain reduces to
\begin{equation}
    K = \frac{P}{P + R},
    \label{eq:qff_gain_scalar}
\end{equation}
which ranges between zero, when the prediction is much more accurate than the
measurement, and one, when the measurement is much more accurate than the prediction.
Increasing $\mathbf{Q}_k$ increases $P$ and therefore $K$, so that the corrected state
moves away from the prediction and towards the measurement. Through the off-diagonal
terms of $\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}$, the effect is not limited to the measured position. The
process noise of the CCA model acts on the acceleration and on the curvature
(Section~\ref{subsec:tt_cca}), and a larger uncertainty on these states allows the
correction of the position to be transferred more quickly to the speed and to the
acceleration, which are not measured directly by the LiDARs and by the camera.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5/q_high_vs_baseline.pdf}
    \caption{Speed and acceleration estimated with the nominal and with an increased process
    noise. Session of 5 September 2026, Imola.}
    \label{fig:qff_highq}
\end{figure}

By varying $\mathbf{Q}_k$, the filter is therefore moved along a trade-off between reactivity
and noise, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_highq}, which compares the estimate obtained with the
nominal process noise and with an increased one, $q_a = 500$, over two braking maneuvers. With
the increased process noise, the larger gain makes the filter follow the changes of maneuver
quickly, with an acceleration close to the ground truth at the beginning and at the end of the
braking phases, but in the phases at constant acceleration it also follows the noise of the
measurements, and the estimate of the acceleration becomes noisier without any benefit. With
the nominal process noise, instead, the estimate is smoother, but it follows the transitions
with a visible delay, which is also reflected on the speed. Neither value is therefore adequate
over the whole lap, since the dynamics of the vehicle alternates slowly varying phases with
abrupt transitions between traction and braking, and the idea of the approach is to alternate
between the two values of the process noise according to the detection of the maneuver, as
formulated in Section~\ref{sec:qff_formulation} after the tuning of the nominal value.

\section{Nominal Process Noise Tuning}
\label{sec:qff_nominal}

Preliminary to the feed-forward approach is the tuning of the nominal value
$\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{nom}}$ of the process noise, i.e.\ of the spectral densities $q_a$
and $q_\kappa$ of Section~\ref{subsec:tt_cca}. The choice is made by testing different
values of the process noise and selecting the one that gives a good starting point on the
trade-off introduced in the previous section, between the reactivity of the filter to
the changes of the dynamics and the noise of the estimate. This value is the baseline of
the feed-forward approach, in which it is multiplied by an inflation factor during the
maneuvers.

\section{Feed-Forward Formulation}
\label{sec:qff_formulation}

Following the idea of Section~\ref{sec:qff_idea}, the process noise covariance of the filter is
switched between the nominal value of Section~\ref{sec:qff_nominal}, suited to the phases in
which the dynamics of the opponent changes slowly, and an inflated one, which makes the filter
reactive during the maneuvers. The switch is driven by a maneuver flag $f_k$, which is
activated when a maneuver is detected and deactivated when it is over,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{Q}_k =
    \begin{cases}
        \mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{nom}} & \text{if } f_k = 0, \\
        \lambda\,\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{nom}} & \text{if } f_k = 1,
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:qff_switch}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda > 1$ is the inflation factor. The maneuver is detected from the jerk of the
opponent, i.e.\ the time derivative of its longitudinal acceleration, which directly measures
the departure of the actual motion from the constant acceleration assumed by the CCA model,
being small in the phases that the model describes correctly and large at the beginning and at
the end of the maneuvers.

\subsection{Maneuver Detection Logic}
\label{subsec:qff_detection}

To use the jerk as the variable for the detection of the maneuver, an estimate of it has to be
obtained first, since it is not measured by the sensors, and two approaches are possible.

The first consists in augmenting the state of the filter with the jerk, which turns the CCA
model into a constant-jerk model. This solution is not pursued, since it would require a new
formulation of the motion model and of its Jacobians, together with a new characterization of
the process noise, which would act on the derivative of the jerk, and of its propagation to the
other states through the discretization of Section~\ref{subsec:tt_cca}, and since the jerk,
not being measured by any sensor, could be corrected through the speed measured by the RaDAR
only after a double integration.

The second approach, adopted in this work, consists in obtaining the jerk by differentiating
and filtering the estimated acceleration, and it is preferred since it can be pursued without
modifying the motion model of the filter. Two families of filters are considered, time-domain
and frequency-based ones.

\paragraph{Time-domain filters.} In this family, the differentiated acceleration is
filtered by interpolating the signal with a polynomial of degree $n$ over a moving window
of $m$ samples. The degree of the polynomial sets the degrees of freedom of the fit,
while the length of the window sets the number of samples used for the fitting, and
therefore the amount of smoothing. The classical filter of this family is the
Savitzky--Golay filter. Polynomial filters, however, typically optimize the estimate at
the center of the window. In a real-time application, this would require using both the
past and the future samples of the signal, which are not available online. For this
reason, this family is discarded, and the frequency-based filters are adopted.

\paragraph{Frequency-based filters.} In this family, the acceleration is differentiated
and filtered with a low-pass filter. Both filters can be written as the product of the
derivative and of a low-pass filter $L(s)$,
\begin{equation}
    H(s) = s\,L(s).
    \label{eq:qff_diff}
\end{equation}
Among the filters considered is the first-order low-pass filter, which combined with the
derivative gives the dirty derivative, i.e.\ a differentiator band-limited by a pole with
time constant $\tau$,
\begin{equation}
    H_1(s) = \frac{s}{1 + \tau s}.
    \label{eq:qff_dirty}
\end{equation}
The second is the Butterworth filter of the second order, with cutoff frequency
$\omega_c$, whose combination with the derivative gives
\begin{equation}
    H_2(s) = \frac{s\,\omega_c^2}{s^2 + \sqrt{2}\,\omega_c\, s + \omega_c^2}.
    \label{eq:qff_butter}
\end{equation}
The Bode diagrams of the two transfer functions are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_bode}. Both
behave as a differentiator below the cutoff frequency, whereas above it $|H_1|$ tends to the
constant $1/\tau$ and $|H_2|$ decreases by \SI{20}{dB} per decade. Being of the second order,
the Butterworth filter therefore guarantees a better roll-off, i.e.\ a stronger attenuation of
the disturbances above the cutoff frequency, while keeping a flat pass band, and it is for this
reason adopted to estimate the jerk.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/ch5/jerk_filters_bode.pdf}
    \caption{Bode diagrams of the dirty derivative $H_1$ and of the Butterworth filter $H_2$.}
    \label{fig:qff_bode}
\end{figure}

Once the jerk is estimated, the maneuver is detected through a hysteresis logic on its
magnitude, based on two thresholds $j_{\mathrm{on}} > j_{\mathrm{off}}$ and described by the
finite state machine of Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_fsm}. The flag is activated when the magnitude of the
jerk exceeds the upper threshold, and it is deactivated only when it falls below the lower
one. Between the two thresholds, the flag keeps its previous state, so that small oscillations
of the jerk around a single threshold do not switch it repeatedly, which limits the false
detections and the chattering of the flag.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \input{figures/ch5/tikz/qff_fsm.tex}
    \caption{Finite state machine of the maneuver flag.}
    \label{fig:qff_fsm}
\end{figure}

The thresholds are chosen by considering the jerk estimated from the ground truth, by looking
at its values when the vehicle is on a straight, i.e.\ with a slowly varying dynamics, and when
its dynamics changes, at the beginning and at the end of the maneuvers, so that the latter
exceed the upper threshold while the former remain mostly below the lower one. This leads to
$j_{\mathrm{on}} = \SI{15}{m/s^3}$ and $j_{\mathrm{off}} = \SI{5}{m/s^3}$.

\subsection{Implementation}
\label{subsec:qff_implementation}
\label{sec:qff_implementation}

Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_scheme} shows how the feed-forward approach is implemented in the tracking
module.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \input{figures/ch5/tikz/qff_scheme.tex}
    \caption{Architecture of the feed-forward process noise.}
    \label{fig:qff_scheme}
\end{figure}

Two filters run in parallel for each track, both based on the same motion model, i.e.\ the EKF
with the CCA model of Chapter~\ref{ch:tracking}. The first, main filter publishes the state of
the opponent on the history passed to the planning module, and its process noise is switched
by the hysteresis logic. The second has a constant and increased process noise, and it is
introduced only to estimate the jerk and to manage the maneuver flag.

This second filter is needed for two reasons. The first is the delay of the detection. If the
jerk were derived from the main filter, which adopts the nominal process noise until the flag
is activated, its acceleration would follow the transitions with the delay shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_highq}, and the activation threshold would be exceeded only when the
maneuver is already in progress. The jerk is therefore derived from the filter with the
increased process noise, which is the most reactive and the closest to the ground truth at the
changes of maneuver. The second reason is the coupling between the flag and the estimate. If
the jerk were derived from the main filter, the activation of the flag would inflate the
process noise of the same filter, making its acceleration noisier and its jerk larger, which
would in turn keep the flag active, so that the detection would depend on its own output in a
feedback loop. Since the process noise of the dedicated filter does not depend on the flag, the
detection is decoupled from the switching, and the process noise of the main filter is set in
feed-forward.

The inflation factor is set to $\lambda = 100$. Since the nominal spectral density of the
acceleration is $q_a = 5$, during the maneuvers the main filter adopts the same process noise
as the filter used for the detection, i.e.\ the increased process noise of
Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_highq}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:qff_results}

The feed-forward approach is evaluated on the session recorded on 5 September 2026 at Imola,
with the parameters of Section~\ref{subsec:qff_implementation}. The estimate of the main
filter with the feed-forward process noise is compared with those of the nominal filter and of
the filter with the increased process noise, which represent the two behaviors between which
the method switches. The comparison is first carried out on a sequence of corners and then on
the detail of a single braking, and in both cases the estimate of the speed and of the
acceleration is followed by the estimated jerk, compared with the one of the ground truth and
with the thresholds of the hysteresis.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5/qff_overview.pdf}
    \caption{Speed and acceleration estimated with the feed-forward process noise over a sequence of corners. Session of 5 September 2026, Imola.}
    \label{fig:qff_overview}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_overview} shows a sequence that alternates braking phases with phases at
almost constant speed. In the latter, the feed-forward estimate overlaps the nominal one and
preserves its smoothness, while the filter with the increased process noise follows the noise
of the measurements. In the two braking phases between \SI{730}{s} and \SI{740}{s}, the
feed-forward estimate departs from the nominal one and approximates the ground truth better. In
the braking at about \SI{760}{s}, instead, it remains close to the nominal estimate, with the
same delay with respect to the ground truth, and it departs from it only in the following phase
of traction.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5/qff_jerk_overview.pdf}
    \caption{Acceleration and estimated jerk, with the activation thresholds of the flag, for the maneuvers of Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_overview}. Session of 5 September 2026, Imola.}
    \label{fig:qff_jerk_overview}
\end{figure}

The instants at which the flag is activated can be read from Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_jerk_overview},
which shows the estimated jerk for the same maneuvers. On the straight, the estimated jerk
remains below the thresholds and the flag is inactive, which is why the feed-forward estimate
coincides with the nominal one. In the braking phases between \SI{730}{s} and \SI{740}{s}, the
estimated jerk exceeds the upper threshold close to the onset of the maneuvers. At about
\SI{760}{s}, instead, the jerk of the ground truth exhibits a sharp peak at the onset of the
braking, while the estimated jerk reaches the upper threshold only a few tenths of a second later, which
explains why the feed-forward estimate follows the nominal one during the braking. The figure
also shows isolated peaks of the estimated jerk, e.g.\ at about \SI{739}{s}, which exceed the
thresholds while the jerk of the ground truth remains small, and which therefore activate the
flag outside the maneuvers.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5/qff_braking_detail.pdf}
    \caption{Detail of a braking phase. Session of 5 September 2026, Imola.}
    \label{fig:qff_braking_detail}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_braking_detail} shows the detail of a braking in which the maneuver is
detected at its onset. The feed-forward estimate starts to decrease with a slight delay with
respect to the filter with the increased process noise, but well before the nominal one, whose
delay is accumulated in the speed, which is underestimated at the end of the braking. The
feed-forward estimate of the speed, instead, remains close to the ground truth during the whole
maneuver.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5/qff_jerk.pdf}
    \caption{Acceleration and estimated jerk, with the activation thresholds of the flag, for the braking of Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_braking_detail}. Session of 5 September 2026, Imola.}
    \label{fig:qff_jerk}
\end{figure}

In the corresponding jerk, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_jerk}, the main peak of the estimate
follows the one of the ground truth with a slight delay, which is the same delay observed
between the feed-forward estimate and the one of the filter with the increased process noise.
After the braking, some peaks of the estimated jerk exceed the upper threshold while the jerk of
the ground truth remains small, activating the flag also in this case outside the maneuver.

These results show that the approach works as intended. The flag remains inactive in the phases
at constant acceleration and is activated in the braking and traction phases, so that the filter
with the feed-forward process noise lies between the nominal filter and the one with the
increased process noise, keeping the smoothness of the former where the dynamics changes slowly
and approaching the reactivity of the latter during the maneuvers. The cases in which the
approach is less effective, i.e.\ the late activation at about \SI{760}{s}, the slight delay
of Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_braking_detail} and the activations outside the maneuvers, share a common
origin in the jerk estimator. Since the jerk is obtained by differentiating the acceleration of
the filter with the increased process noise, it inherits the delay with which this filter
follows the changes of the dynamics, which is transferred to the activation of the flag, while
the differentiation amplifies part of its noise, which produces the spurious activations.
