\chapter{Q Feed-Forward}
\label{ch:qff}

The motion model of the tracker, introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:tt_cca}, assumes that
the opponent moves with constant curvature and constant longitudinal acceleration over
each prediction interval. This assumption holds well when the dynamics of the opponent
changes slowly, e.g.\ on a straight or in the middle of a corner, but it is violated at
the beginning and at the end of the maneuvers. When the opponent starts braking, or
passes from braking to traction, the acceleration changes abruptly, and the actual
motion departs from the one predicted by the CCA model. In these conditions, the dynamic
model is no longer correct and the quality of the estimate degrades. The filter relies on
the motion model more than it should, and it follows the new dynamics only after several
measurements have confirmed it, so that the estimate lags behind the true state of the
opponent.

To recover the quality of the estimate during the maneuvers, a feed-forward approach on
the process noise covariance is implemented. Its idea and rationale are explained in
Section~\ref{sec:qff_idea}. Section~\ref{sec:qff_nominal} describes the preliminary tuning
of the nominal process noise, which is the starting value of the method.
Section~\ref{sec:qff_formulation} presents the feed-forward logic, and
Section~\ref{sec:qff_jerk} the estimation of the jerk on which it is based. Finally,
Section~\ref{sec:qff_implementation} describes the implementation, and
Section~\ref{sec:qff_results} compares the resulting estimate with the one of the nominal
filter.

\section{The Idea}
\label{sec:qff_idea}

The approach acts on the process noise covariance $\mathbf{Q}_k$, which represents the
uncertainty of the motion model, i.e.\ how much the actual motion of the opponent is
expected to differ from the predicted one. In the prediction step, the process noise is added to the covariance propagated through
the motion model,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{P}_{k+1|k} = \mathbf{F}_k\,\mathbf{P}_{k|k}\,\mathbf{F}_k^\top + \mathbf{Q}_k,
    \label{eq:qff_pred_cov}
\end{equation}
and the predicted covariance enters the Kalman gain of the correction step,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{K}_{k+1} = \mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}\,\mathbf{H}_{k+1}^\top
    \left(\mathbf{H}_{k+1}\,\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}\,\mathbf{H}_{k+1}^\top + \mathbf{R}_{k+1}\right)^{-1},
    \label{eq:qff_gain}
\end{equation}
as in~\eqref{eq:tt_prediction} and~\eqref{eq:tt_update}. Considering a single component,
with a predicted variance $P$ and a measurement noise variance $R$, the gain reduces to
\begin{equation}
    K = \frac{P}{P + R},
    \label{eq:qff_gain_scalar}
\end{equation}
which ranges between zero, when the prediction is much more accurate than the
measurement, and one, when the measurement is much more accurate than the prediction.
Increasing $\mathbf{Q}_k$ increases $P$ and therefore $K$, so that the corrected state
moves away from the prediction and towards the measurement. The process noise is
therefore the parameter that sets how much the filter trusts the motion model with respect
to the measurements.

Through the off-diagonal terms of $\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}$, the effect is not limited to the
measured position. The process noise of the CCA model acts on the acceleration and on the
curvature (Section~\ref{subsec:tt_cca}), and a larger uncertainty on these states allows
the correction of the position to be transferred more quickly to the speed and to the
acceleration, which are not measured directly by the LiDARs and by the camera.

A constant process noise has to balance two opposite requirements. A small value gives a
smooth estimate when the dynamics of the opponent changes slowly, but a slow response at
the beginning of a maneuver. A large value gives a fast response during the maneuvers,
but a noisy estimate in the rest of the lap. For this reason, the process noise is not
increased permanently, but only when a maneuver is detected, through a feed-forward logic
based on the estimate of the jerk of the opponent. In this way, the filter adapts its
process noise to the current conditions.

\section{Nominal Process Noise Tuning}
\label{sec:qff_nominal}

As shown in the previous section, the process noise directly affects the estimate of the
filter. Its nominal value $\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{nom}}$, i.e.\ the spectral densities $q_a$
and $q_\kappa$ of Section~\ref{subsec:tt_cca}, has therefore to be chosen to balance how
much the filter corrects the prediction with the measurements, i.e.\ how reactive it is
to the changes of the dynamics, against how noisy the estimate is. This value is the
starting point of the feed-forward approach, in which it is multiplied by an inflation
factor during the maneuvers.

\section{Feed-Forward Formulation}
\label{sec:qff_formulation}

The process noise covariance is switched between its nominal value and an inflated one,
depending on a flag that indicates whether the opponent is performing a maneuver,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{Q}_k =
    \begin{cases}
        \mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{nom}} & \text{if } f_k = 0, \\
        \lambda\,\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{nom}} & \text{if } f_k = 1,
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:qff_switch}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{nom}}$ is the nominal process noise of
Section~\ref{sec:qff_nominal}, $\lambda > 1$ the inflation factor and $f_k$ the maneuver
flag. When the flag is active, the filter becomes more reactive and follows the
measurements more closely. When it is not active, the nominal behavior of the filter is
recovered.

The maneuver detection is based on the jerk $j_k$ of the opponent, i.e.\ the time
derivative of its longitudinal acceleration. The jerk is the natural candidate for this
purpose. Being the derivative of the acceleration, its magnitude directly indicates
whether the acceleration is changing, i.e.\ whether the opponent is performing a maneuver
that the CCA model, with its constant acceleration, cannot predict.

To limit the false detections and the chattering of the flag, the activation is based on
two thresholds, $j_{\mathrm{on}} > j_{\mathrm{off}}$, with a hysteresis logic, described
by the finite state machine of Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_fsm}. The flag is activated when the
magnitude of the jerk exceeds the upper threshold, and it is deactivated only when it
falls below the lower one. Between the two thresholds, the flag keeps its previous state,
so that small oscillations of the jerk around a single threshold do not switch it
repeatedly. The thresholds are set to $j_{\mathrm{on}} = \SI{15}{m/s^3}$ and
$j_{\mathrm{off}} = \SI{10}{m/s^3}$, and the inflation factor to
$\lambda = $~\TODO{value}.

\section{Jerk Estimation}
\label{sec:qff_jerk}

The jerk is not measured directly by the sensors, and it is therefore derived from the
estimated acceleration.

Since the jerk is the derivative of a noisy signal, the differentiation has to be
combined with a filtering, which limits the amplification of the high-frequency noise.
Two families of filters are considered, frequency-based and time-domain ones.

\paragraph{Frequency-based filters.} In this family, the acceleration is essentially
differentiated and filtered with a low-pass filter. Two filters are considered. The first
is the first-order low-pass filter, which combined with the derivative gives the dirty
derivative, i.e.\ a differentiator band-limited by a pole with time constant $\tau$,
\begin{equation}
    H(s) = \frac{s}{1 + \tau s}.
    \label{eq:qff_dirty}
\end{equation}
The second is the Butterworth filter, which is essentially a second-order filter, with
magnitude response
\begin{equation}
    |H(j\omega)|^2 = \frac{1}{1 + \left(\omega/\omega_c\right)^{2n}},
    \qquad n = 2,
    \label{eq:qff_butter}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_c$ is the cutoff frequency, followed by the numerical derivative. Being of
the second order, the Butterworth filter guarantees a faster roll-off than the first-order
filter, and therefore a better attenuation of the disturbances above the cutoff
frequency, while keeping a flat pass band.

\TODO{Figure: magnitude response of the first-order low-pass filter and of the Butterworth
filter.}

\paragraph{Time-domain filters.} In this family, the differentiated acceleration is
filtered by interpolating the signal with a polynomial of degree $n$ over a moving window
of $m$ samples. The degree of the polynomial sets the degrees of freedom of the fit,
while the length of the window sets the number of samples used for the fitting, and
therefore the amount of smoothing. The classical filter of this family is the
Savitzky--Golay filter. Polynomial filters, however, typically optimize the estimate at
the center of the window. In a real-time application, this would require using both the
past and the future samples of the signal, which are not available online. For this
reason, this family is discarded.

The jerk estimated with the dirty derivative, with $\tau = \SI{0.064}{s}$, and with the
Butterworth filter, with $f_c = \SI{2.5}{Hz}$, is compared with the jerk obtained by
differentiating the acceleration of the ground truth with a Savitzky--Golay filter, which
can be applied since the comparison is carried out offline. The comparison also includes
the two thresholds of Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_fsm}.

Both estimates detect the main transitions of the acceleration, i.e.\ the beginning and
the end of the braking phases, where the jerk exceeds the upper threshold. Between the
maneuvers, the noise of the estimate remains mostly below the lower threshold, but some

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:qff_implementation}

The feed-forward approach is implemented with two filters that evolve in parallel, for
each track, and process the same measurements. The first is the classical filter of
Chapter~\ref{ch:tracking}, whose process noise is switched by the hysteresis logic of
Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_fsm}. Its state is the one published to the planning module. The second
is used only to estimate the jerk and, therefore, to activate and deactivate the maneuver
flag.

The jerk is estimated from a parallel filter, and not from the first one, because of the
delay of the latter. With its process noise tuned at the nominal value, the first filter
reacts to the changes of the dynamics with the delay that the method aims to reduce. A
jerk computed from its acceleration would inherit this delay, and it would not allow the
flag to be activated when the maneuver begins. The parallel filter, instead, has the same
structure, i.e.\ the EKF with the CCA model, but its process noise is permanently
inflated. Its acceleration follows the changes of the dynamics with a smaller delay, at
the cost of a noisier estimate, and the jerk derived from it can activate the flag at the
beginning of the maneuver. Moreover, since the process noise of the parallel filter does
not depend on the flag, the jerk that drives the flag is not affected by the switching of
the process noise of the first filter. The complete procedure is reported in Algorithm~\ref{alg:qff}.

\begin{algorithm}[!htbp]
\caption{Feed-forward process noise, for a single track.}
\label{alg:qff}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE nominal process noise $\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{nom}}$, inflation factor $\lambda$,
thresholds $j_{\mathrm{on}}$ and $j_{\mathrm{off}}$, previous flag $f_{k-1}$
\FORALL{measurements $\mathbf{z}_{k+1}$ associated with the track}
    \STATE update the parallel filter with $\mathbf{z}_{k+1}$ \COMMENT{higher process noise}
    \STATE $a_{k+1} \leftarrow$ acceleration of the parallel filter
    \STATE $j_{k+1} \leftarrow$ filtered derivative of $a_{k+1}$ \COMMENT{\eqref{eq:qff_dirty} or \eqref{eq:qff_butter}}
    \IF{$|j_{k+1}| > j_{\mathrm{on}}$}
        \STATE $f_{k+1} \leftarrow 1$
    \ELSIF{$|j_{k+1}| < j_{\mathrm{off}}$}
        \STATE $f_{k+1} \leftarrow 0$
    \ELSE
        \STATE $f_{k+1} \leftarrow f_k$ \COMMENT{hysteresis, Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_fsm}}
    \ENDIF
    \STATE $\mathbf{Q}_k \leftarrow \lambda\,\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{nom}}$ \textbf{if} $f_{k+1} = 1$ \textbf{else} $\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{nom}}$
    \STATE predict the main filter with $\mathbf{Q}_k$ \COMMENT{\eqref{eq:qff_pred_cov}}
    \STATE correct the main filter with $\mathbf{z}_{k+1}$ \COMMENT{\eqref{eq:qff_gain}}
\ENDFOR
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Results}
\label{sec:qff_results}

The feed-forward approach is evaluated on session 08/03\_Kinetiz, by comparing the
estimate of the main filter with the feed-forward process noise against the one of the
nominal filter with the CCA model.

\TODO{Figure: speed and acceleration with the nominal and the feed-forward process noise
over a sequence of corners, with the intervals of active maneuver flag.}

The flag is activated at the beginning of the braking phases and at the transition from
braking to traction. In these intervals, the estimate with the feed-forward process noise
follows the change of the acceleration earlier than the nominal one, and the lag of the
speed at the end of each braking phase is reduced. The effect is more evident in the
braking phases, in which the acceleration of the opponent drops from about
\SI{5}{m/s^2} to \SI{-11}{m/s^2} in a few tenths of a second. The nominal filter follows
this change gradually over about one second, while, once the flag is activated, the filter
with the feed-forward process noise reaches the new value of the acceleration much
earlier. As a consequence, its estimate of the speed remains closer to the ground truth
during the whole braking phase.

\TODO{Figure: detail of a braking phase.}

The feed-forward approach is also compared with a filter in which the process noise is
permanently increased. The constant process noise gives a similar reactivity during the
maneuvers, but its acceleration is noisier in the phases in which the dynamics of the
opponent changes slowly. The feed-forward approach, instead, increases the process noise
only when it is needed, and keeps the nominal behavior of the filter in the rest of the
lap.

\TODO{Figure: comparison with a constant increased process noise.}

Overall, the feed-forward process noise makes the filter more reactive, and improves the
estimate of the acceleration and of the speed during the maneuvers, with a smaller lag
with respect to the ground truth.

The quality of the jerk estimate is the main limitation of the method. It depends on the
quality of the acceleration of the parallel filter and, therefore, on the accuracy of the
measurements, above all of the range rate of the RaDAR. Moreover, the jerk is derived from
the speed through a double differentiation, so it inherits the delay of the acceleration
estimate and amplifies its noise. The thresholds remain the only parameter
on the delay of the detection. Lowering them anticipates the activation of the flag, but
also exposes the filter to false activations, during which the estimate becomes noisier
without any benefit.
