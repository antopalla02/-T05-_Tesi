\chapter{Q Feed-Forward}
\label{ch:qff}

The motion model of the tracker, introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:tt_cca}, assumes that
the opponent moves with constant curvature and constant longitudinal acceleration over
each prediction interval. This assumption holds well when the dynamics of the opponent
changes slowly, e.g.\ on a straight, but it is violated during breaking and traction phased. In these, the model is not describing the real behavior of the opponent and, therefore, the quality of the estimate degrades.

This phenomenon is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_cca_baseline}, which compares the speed and the
acceleration estimated by the filter with the CCA model with the
ground truth over two braking maneuvers. At the onset of each braking, the estimated
acceleration departs from the ground truth with a delay of several tenths of a second, and the
lag accumulated in this interval is reflected on the speed, which is overestimated during the
braking and underestimated at its end.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/ch5/cca_baseline.pdf}
    \caption{Speed and acceleration estimated with the CCA model and the nominal process
    noise. Session of 5 September 2026, Imola.}
    \label{fig:qff_cca_baseline}
\end{figure}

Different classes of approaches  have been proposed to recover the correctness of the estimate, the first one, is the multiple model one. The fundamental assumption of this class is that, once a set of motion models has been
defined, the dynamic conditions of the vehicle are described at each instant by at least one of
the models of the set. The models are therefore chosen so that each of them represents a
specific operating condition of the vehicle, such as a phase at constant speed, a braking or a
corner, and, since the vehicle switches between these conditions during the race, they are
evolved in parallel, each through a dedicated filter, while the estimate is obtained as the
fusion of their outputs. In the classical formulation, the filters evolve independently and
their estimates are only combined at the output, weighted by model probabilities computed from
the likelihood of the innovations. The Interacting Multiple Model (IMM) filter extends this
formulation by letting the filters interact, since at each step their initial conditions are
obtained by mixing the estimates of all the models according to the transition probabilities
between them, which allows the estimate to follow the switches between the operating conditions
of the vehicle. These approaches, however, come at the cost of a higher computational load and
of a larger number of parameters to tune, such as the transition probabilities between the
models.

Another class of approaches is based on maneuver detection. An indicator is monitored to
identify when the dynamics of the opponent changes and, once this change is detected, the
behavior of the filter is adapted, switching discretely from one condition to another. In this
chapter, a method belonging to this class is presented, which adapts the process noise
covariance matrix following the detection, i.e.\ in feed-forward, hence its name.

Section~\ref{sec:qff_idea} explains the idea of the approach, and
Section~\ref{sec:qff_nominal} describes the preliminary tuning of the nominal process noise,
the starting value of the method. Section~\ref{sec:qff_formulation} presents the
feed-forward formulation and its implementation in the tracker. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:qff_results}
compares the resulting estimate with the one of the nominal filter.

\section{The Idea}
\label{sec:qff_idea}

The idea behind the approach starts from the gain of the Kalman filter and from the way in
which the process noise covariance $\mathbf{Q}_k$ enters the correction step. The latter, in the prediction step, is added
to the covariance propagated through the motion model,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{P}_{k+1|k} = \mathbf{F}_k\,\mathbf{P}_{k|k}\,\mathbf{F}_k^\top + \mathbf{Q}_k,
    \label{eq:qff_pred_cov}
\end{equation}
which in turn determines the Kalman gain of the correction step,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{K}_{k+1} = \mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}\,\mathbf{H}_{k+1}^\top
    \left(\mathbf{H}_{k+1}\,\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}\,\mathbf{H}_{k+1}^\top + \mathbf{R}_{k+1}\right)^{-1},
    \label{eq:qff_gain}
\end{equation}
as in~\eqref{eq:tt_prediction} and~\eqref{eq:tt_update}. Considering a single component, with a
predicted variance $P$ and a measurement noise variance $R$, the gain reduces to
\begin{equation}
    K = \frac{P}{P + R},
    \label{eq:qff_gain_scalar}
\end{equation}
which ranges between zero, when the prediction is much more accurate than the measurement, and
one, when the measurement is much more accurate than the prediction. Increasing $\mathbf{Q}_k$ therefore increases $P$ and $K$, so that at each correction step the
predicted estimate is moved  towards the measurement. With a small
$\mathbf{Q}_k$ the opposite occurs, since the motion model is considered accurate, the gain
remains small and the corrected estimate stays close to the predicted one.
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5/q_high_vs_baseline.pdf}
    \caption{Speed and acceleration estimated with the nominal and with an increased process
    noise. Session of 5 September 2026, Imola.}
    \label{fig:qff_highq}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_highq} shows this behavior, comparing over two braking maneuvers two
different tunings of the filter, the first with an increased process noise, $q_a = 500$, and the
second with the nominal one of Section~\ref{sec:qff_nominal}. During the maneuvers, the larger
gain of the first tuning makes the filter more reactive, so that its estimate follows the
braking phases more closely, whereas in the phases at constant acceleration the nominal tuning
is more representative, since it filters the noise of the measurements through a prediction in
which it is more confident and therefore returns a considerably smoother estimate. The two
tunings thus turn out to be complementary, the first capturing the changes of maneuver well at
the price of a noisy estimate, and the second providing a more stable estimate at the price of
a larger delay.

Overall, what emerges is that a fixed tuning of the process noise cannot adequately describe a
dynamics that alternates slowly varying phases with abrupt transitions between traction and
braking. From this consideration arises the feed-forward approach, which aims to combine the
advantages of both tunings by starting from the nominal process noise, keeping it as long as
the CCA model correctly describes the dynamics of the opponent, and adapting $\mathbf{Q}_k$
through a multiplicative factor when a maneuver begins.

\section{Nominal Process Noise Tuning}
\label{sec:qff_nominal}

A preliminary step in the development of the feed-forward approach is the tuning of the nominal
process noise covariance $\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{nom}}$, defined by the spectral densities $q_a$
and $q_\kappa$ introduced in Section~\ref{subsec:tt_cca}. The objective is to identify a
suitable compromise between the responsiveness of the filter to changes in the opponent's
dynamics and the smoothness of its estimate, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:qff_idea}.

The tuning is performed by comparing the estimates obtained with different values of the
acceleration noise spectral density $q_a$, while keeping $q_\kappa$ fixed.
Figs.~\ref{fig:qff_tuning_braking} and~\ref{fig:qff_tuning_straight} show the estimated speed
and acceleration for three representative values, $q_a = 1$, $5$ and $50$, during a braking
maneuver and on a straight, respectively.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5/cca_tuning_braking.pdf}
    \caption{Speed and acceleration estimated with different values of $q_a$ during a braking.
    Session of 5 September 2026, Imola.}
    \label{fig:qff_tuning_braking}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5/cca_tuning_straight.pdf}
    \caption{Speed and acceleration estimated with different values of $q_a$ on a straight.
    Session of 5 September 2026, Imola.}
    \label{fig:qff_tuning_straight}
\end{figure}

Consistently with the considerations of Section~\ref{sec:qff_idea}, increasing $q_a$ improves
the responsiveness of the filter at the expense of a noisier estimate. During the braking, the
acceleration estimated with $q_a = 50$ follows the ground truth with the smallest delay,
whereas with $q_a = 1$ the slower response leads to an accumulated error in the speed, which is
underestimated at the end of the maneuver. On the straight, however, the larger Kalman gain
associated with $q_a = 50$ makes the acceleration estimate considerably noisier, with
pronounced spikes, while the estimates obtained with $q_a = 1$ and $q_a = 5$ remain smooth and
nearly coincident.

The value $q_a = 5$ is therefore selected as the nominal tuning, as it provides a suitable
compromise between the two operating conditions: it improves the response to changes in
acceleration compared with $q_a = 1$, while avoiding the pronounced fluctuations observed with
$q_a = 50$ during steady-state motion.

\section{Feed-Forward Formulation}
\label{sec:qff_formulation}

Following the idea of Section~\ref{sec:qff_idea}, the process noise covariance of the filter is
switched between the nominal value of Section~\ref{sec:qff_nominal}, suited to the phases in
which the dynamics of the opponent changes slowly, and an inflated one, which makes the filter
reactive during the maneuvers. The switch is driven by a maneuver flag $f_k$, which is
activated when a maneuver is detected and deactivated when it is over,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{Q}_k =
    \begin{cases}
        \mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{nom}} & \text{if } f_k = 0, \\
        \lambda\,\mathbf{Q}_{\mathrm{nom}} & \text{if } f_k = 1,
    \end{cases}
    \label{eq:qff_switch}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda > 1$ is the inflation factor. The maneuver is detected from the jerk of the
opponent, i.e.\ the time derivative of its longitudinal acceleration, which directly measures
the departure of the actual motion from the constant acceleration assumed by the CCA model,
being small in the phases that the model describes correctly and large at the beginning and at
the end of the maneuvers.

\subsection{Maneuver Detection Logic}
\label{subsec:qff_detection}

To use the jerk as the variable for the detection of the maneuver, an estimate of it has to be
obtained first, since it is not measured by the sensors, and two approaches are possible.

The first consists in augmenting the state of the filter with the jerk, which turns the CCA
model into a constant-jerk model. This solution is not pursued, since it would require a new
formulation of the motion model and of its Jacobians, together with a new characterization and derivation of
the process noise covariances.
The second approach, adopted in this work, consists in obtaining the jerk by differentiating
and filtering the estimated acceleration. \\ Two families of filtering techniques are considered, time-domain
and frequency-based ones.

\paragraph{Time-domain filters.} In this family, the differentiated acceleration is
filtered by interpolating the signal with a polynomial of degree $n$ over a moving window
of $m$ samples. The degree of the polynomial sets the degrees of freedom of the fit,
while the length of the window sets the number of samples used for the fitting, and
therefore the amount of smoothing. The classical filter of this family is the
Savitzky--Golay filter. Polynomial filters, however, are known to provide the best estimate when used in their
non-causal formulation, i.e.\ when the estimate is computed at the center of the window, using
both past and future samples of the signal, and, therefore, cannot be adopted in a real-time application, which is the reason why they are discarded in favor of the frequency-based ones.

\paragraph{Frequency-based filters.} In this family, the filter is designed in the frequency
domain. A differentiator is used to obtain the jerk from the estimated acceleration, and it is
complemented by a low-pass filter $L(s)$, which removes the noise outside the band of
interest, so that the resulting filter can be written as
\begin{equation}
    H(s) = s\,L(s).
    \label{eq:qff_diff}
\end{equation}
Two low-pass filters are considered. The first is a first-order filter, whose combination
with the derivative gives the dirty derivative, i.e.\ a differentiator whose gain is limited
at high frequency by a pole with time constant $\tau$,
\begin{equation}
    H_1(s) = \frac{s}{1 + \tau s}.
    \label{eq:qff_dirty}
\end{equation}
The second is a second-order Butterworth filter with cutoff frequency $\omega_c$, which
gives
\begin{equation}
    H_2(s) = \frac{s\,\omega_c^2}{s^2 + \sqrt{2}\,\omega_c\, s + \omega_c^2}.
    \label{eq:qff_butter}
\end{equation}
The Bode diagrams of the two transfer functions are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_bode}. Both
behave as a differentiator below the cutoff frequency, whereas above it $|H_1|$ tends to the
constant $1/\tau$ and $|H_2|$ decreases by \SI{20}{dB} per decade. Being of the second order,
the Butterworth filter therefore guarantees a better roll-off, i.e.\ a stronger attenuation of
the disturbances above the cutoff frequency, and it is for this reason adopted to estimate the jerk.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/ch5/jerk_filters_bode.pdf}
    \caption{Bode diagrams of the dirty derivative $H_1$ and of the Butterworth filter $H_2$.}
    \label{fig:qff_bode}
\end{figure}

Once the jerk is estimated, the maneuver is detected through a hysteresis logic on its
magnitude, based on two thresholds $j_{\mathrm{on}} > j_{\mathrm{off}}$ and described by the
finite state machine of Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_fsm}. The flag is activated when the magnitude of the jerk exceeds the upper threshold and
deactivated only when it falls below the lower one, while between the two it keeps its previous
state, so that small oscillations around a single threshold do not switch it repeatedly, which
limits false detections and chattering.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \input{figures/ch5/tikz/qff_fsm.tex}
    \caption{Finite state machine of the maneuver flag.}
    \label{fig:qff_fsm}
\end{figure}

The thresholds are chosen on the jerk of the ground truth, by comparing its values on the
straights, where the dynamics varies slowly, with those at the beginning and at the end of the
maneuvers, so that the latter exceed the upper threshold while the former remain mostly below
the lower one. This leads to $j_{\mathrm{on}} = \SI{15}{m/s^3}$ and $j_{\mathrm{off}} = \SI{5}{m/s^3}$.
\subsection{Implementation}
\label{subsec:qff_implementation}
\label{sec:qff_implementation}

Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_scheme} shows how the feed-forward approach is implemented in the tracking
module.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \input{figures/ch5/tikz/qff_scheme.tex}
    \caption{Architecture of the feed-forward process noise.}
    \label{fig:qff_scheme}
\end{figure}

Two filters run in parallel for each track, both based on the same motion model, i.e.\ the EKF
with the CCA model of Chapter~\ref{ch:tracking}. The first, main filter publishes the state of
the opponent on the history passed to the planning module, and its process noise is switched
by the hysteresis logic. The second has a constant and increased process noise, and it is
introduced only to estimate the jerk and to manage the maneuver flag.

The reasons for introducing a second filter, rather than using the main one, are mainly two.
The first is that, if the jerk were estimated from the main filter, the reactivity of the
estimate could not be recovered. Since the jerk is derived from the acceleration, any delay of
the latter is directly transferred to the former, so that the flag would be activated late,
when the maneuver is already in progress. The second filter, instead, is tuned with the
increased process noise, which, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_highq}, makes its acceleration
more reactive at the changes of maneuver and therefore provides a less delayed derivative. The
second reason is the feedback loop that would arise if the flag were driven by the same filter
whose process noise it switches. Once the flag is activated, the process noise of the main
filter is inflated and its acceleration becomes noisier, so that the jerk tends to remain
above the thresholds and the flag is kept active by its own effect on the estimate, regardless
of the actual dynamics of the opponent. Since the process noise of the second filter is
constant, the detection is independent of the state of the flag and decoupled from the
switching, and the process noise of the main filter is set in feed-forward.

The inflation factor is set to $\lambda = 100$. Since the nominal spectral density of the
acceleration is $q_a = 5$, during the maneuvers the main filter adopts the same process noise used by the auxiliary filter,  i.e.\ the increased process noise of
Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_highq}.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:qff_results}

The feed-forward approach is evaluated on the session recorded on 5 September 2026 at Imola,
with the parameters of Sections~\ref{subsec:qff_detection} and~\ref{subsec:qff_implementation}.
The estimate of the main filter is compared with those of the nominal filter and of the filter
with the increased process noise, which bound the two behaviors between which the method
switches. The comparison is carried out first on a sequence of corners and then on a single
braking, and in both cases the estimated kinematic states are complemented by the output of the
detection logic.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5/qff_overview.pdf}
    \caption{Speed and acceleration estimated with the feed-forward process noise over a sequence of corners. Session of 5 September 2026, Imola.}
    \label{fig:qff_overview}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_overview} shows a sequence that alternates braking phases with phases at
almost constant speed. In the latter, the feed-forward estimate coincides with the nominal one
and retains its smoothness, whereas the filter with the increased process noise propagates the
measurement noise into the acceleration. In the two braking phases between \SI{730}{s} and
\SI{740}{s}, the switch to the inflated process noise enables a fast correction of the
acceleration and of the speed, which reduces the lag of the nominal filter and keeps the
estimate bounded by the two references. In the braking at about \SI{760}{s}, instead, the
feed-forward estimate exhibits the same lag as the nominal one during the deceleration
transient, and it differs from it only in the subsequent traction phase.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5/qff_jerk_overview.pdf}
    \caption{Acceleration and estimated jerk, with the activation thresholds of the flag, for the maneuvers of Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_overview}. Session of 5 September 2026, Imola.}
    \label{fig:qff_jerk_overview}
\end{figure}

The switching instants are explained by Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_jerk_overview}, which reports the jerk
estimated by the auxiliary filter together with the ground truth and the hysteresis thresholds.
On the straight, the estimate remains within the thresholds and the nominal process noise is
retained, while in the braking phases between \SI{730}{s} and \SI{740}{s} the upper threshold
is crossed close to the onset of the maneuvers. At about \SI{760}{s}, instead, the estimate lags
the ground truth by a few tenths of a second, which accounts for the delayed switching observed
in Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_overview}. A spurious activation also occurs at about \SI{739}{s}, where
the estimate crosses the thresholds without a corresponding variation of the ground truth.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5/qff_braking_detail.pdf}
    \caption{Detail of a braking phase. Session of 5 September 2026, Imola.}
    \label{fig:qff_braking_detail}
\end{figure}

Analyzing another braking, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_braking_detail}, the same behavior is
observed. During the maneuver, the feed-forward estimate is bounded by the two references and
anticipates the onset of the deceleration with respect to the nominal filter.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch5/qff_jerk.pdf}
    \caption{Acceleration and estimated jerk, with the activation thresholds of the flag, for the braking of Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_braking_detail}. Session of 5 September 2026, Imola.}
    \label{fig:qff_jerk}
\end{figure}

In Fig.~\ref{fig:qff_jerk}, the estimate is consistent with the ground truth but lags it
slightly, and it crosses the activation threshold also in the absence of a maneuver, causing
spurious switching, e.g.\ at about \SI{868}{s}.

The results confirm the effectiveness of the proposed approach in adapting the filter to changes
in the opponent's dynamics. When a maneuver is detected, the increase in process noise allows
the filter to respond more rapidly, whereas, in the absence of an activation, its behavior
remains equivalent to that of the nominal configuration. However, the main limitation lies in
the reactive nature of the strategy: the filter can only adapt once the maneuver has been
detected, making its performance strongly dependent on the timeliness and reliability of the
detection signal.

In the proposed implementation, maneuver detection relies on the estimated jerk, obtained by
differentiating the acceleration provided by the auxiliary filter. Although this acceleration
estimate is more responsive and closer to the ground truth than the nominal one, it inevitably
exhibits some delay due to the causal nature of the filtering process. Additional latency
arises from the jerk estimation itself. Differentiation amplifies high-frequency noise, making
low-pass filtering necessary to obtain a sufficiently stable signal. However, stronger
filtering also introduces greater phase delay, resulting in an unavoidable trade-off between
noise rejection and detection responsiveness.

These limitations are reflected in the experimental results. The combined delay of the
acceleration estimation and jerk filtering postpones maneuver detection, as observed around
\SI{760}{s}, while residual noise can lead to spurious activations even when the opponent's
dynamics is not significantly excited. In this context, the detection threshold represents the
main tuning parameter governing the trade-off between responsiveness and robustness. Lowering
the threshold enables earlier detection and improves the filter's ability to react at the onset
of a maneuver, but also increases its sensitivity to noise, leading to more frequent false
activations. Conversely, a higher threshold reduces spurious detections at the cost of a delayed
response to changes in the opponent's dynamics.
