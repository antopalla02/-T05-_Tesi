\chapter{Experimental Setup and Methodology}
\label{ch:setup}

This chapter describes the vehicles, the sensors and the software on which this work is
built. The research considers two autonomous racing cars: the Dallara AV-24, which competes in
the Indy Autonomous Challenge (IAC), and the Dallara EAV-25, which races in the Abu Dhabi
Autonomous Racing League (A2RL). Both vehicles are equipped with the sensing and computational infrastructure
required to develop, implement, and evaluate the algorithms presented in the following chapters.

Section~\ref{sec:operating_conditions} introduces the two competitions, their racing formats,
and the vehicles employed, with Section~\ref{subsec:iac} devoted to the IAC and the Dallara AV-24
and Section~\ref{subsec:a2rl} to the A2RL and the Dallara EAV-25. Section~\ref{sec:sensors}
describes the operating principles and configurations of the exteroceptive sensors used for
opponent tracking, while Section~\ref{sec:sw_arch} provides an overview of the software
architecture.

\section{Autonomous Racing Competitions}
\label{sec:operating_conditions}

The IAC and A2RL are autonomous racing competitions in which university teams from around the
world develop the software that enables racing cars to drive autonomously. These events bring
autonomous driving into motorsport, subjecting the developed algorithms to particularly
challenging conditions, with vehicles operating close to their dynamic limits and reaching
speeds exceeding \SI{250}{km/h}. In multi-vehicle races, these demands are further increased by
close interactions with opponents and the need to perform racing manoeuvres at high speed. Both
competitions therefore provide a fertile environment for the development of autonomous driving
technologies, enabling the exploration of scenarios that are difficult to reproduce in other
applications.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Indy Autonomous Challenge}
\label{subsec:iac}

The Indy Autonomous Challenge (IAC)~\cite{iac_web} was among the first competitions to bring fully autonomous
racing cars to real circuits. Launched in 2019 with a series of simulation-based
challenges, it held its first on-track event in 2021 at the Indianapolis Motor Speedway. All
participating teams compete using an identical vehicle platform provided by the organisers, with
the competition focusing on the development of the autonomous driving software. Initially, IAC
events were held primarily on oval circuits, including the Indianapolis Motor Speedway, Las Vegas
Motor Speedway, and Texas Motor Speedway. In 2023, the competition expanded to road courses with
its first event at the Autodromo Nazionale Monza, followed by events at WeatherTech Raceway Laguna
Seca in 2025 and 2026 (Fig.~\ref{fig:av24}).

The IAC features both single-vehicle and multi-vehicle competition formats. In single-vehicle
events, such as time trials and qualifying sessions, each car runs independently and is ranked
according to its fastest lap time. These formats primarily challenge the ability of the
autonomous driving system to exploit the vehicle's dynamic capabilities while maintaining
stability and control. Multi-vehicle formats introduce the additional complexity of interactions
between competing vehicles, requiring the autonomous system to perceive and react to the behaviour
of other racing cars.

Among these formats, the passing competition, introduced in 2022, is specifically designed to
challenge autonomous overtaking capabilities. It follows a single-elimination tournament
structure, with teams competing in head-to-head matches. During each match, the vehicles
alternate between the roles of attacker and defender: the defender maintains a prescribed speed
and racing line, while the attacker attempts to complete an overtake within a designated passing
zone. The difficulty progressively increases as the target speed is raised over successive
rounds, until one of the competitors fails to complete the manoeuvre.

\section{Sensors}
\label{sec:sensors}

This section focuses on the exteroceptive sensors mounted on the Dallara AV-24 and EAV-25,
i.e.\ LiDARs, RaDARs and cameras, since they are the sensors on which the perception, and
therefore the Target Tracking addressed in this work, is based. For each type of sensor, the
working principle and the main strengths and limitations are presented first, followed by the
main characteristics and performance of the specific units mounted on each vehicle.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{LiDAR}
\label{subsec:lidar}

A LiDAR (Light Detection and Ranging) is an active sensor that emits laser pulses and
measures the distance of the surfaces that reflect them from the time of flight of each
pulse, $r = c\,\Delta t/2$. A scanning mechanism steers the beam in azimuth and
elevation, and the high pulse repetition rate allows hundreds of thousands of points to
be acquired every second. The output of each scan is a point cloud: a dense
three-dimensional sampling of the surrounding surfaces, with centimetre-level range
precision and an angular resolution of a few hundredths of a degree. From the point cloud
the shape and the size of the objects around the vehicle can be reconstructed.

The quality of the measurements degrades in
adverse weather, since rain, fog and the spray raised by other cars attenuate and
scatter the laser beam. The energy returned by a target also decreases quickly
with the distance and with the reflectivity of its surface, so that the working range
is shorter than that of RaDAR technologies. Finally, a LiDAR measures positions only:
the velocity of a target is not observed directly and has to be inferred from
consecutive scans.

\subsubsection{Dallara AV-24}

\subsection{RaDAR}
\label{subsec:radar}

A RaDAR (Radio Detection and Ranging) is an active sensor that emits radio waves and analyses
the echoes reflected by surrounding objects to estimate their distance and direction. Through
the Doppler effect, it also directly measures the range rate $\dot\rho$, i.e.\ the rate at which
the distance between the sensor and the target changes. This quantity is related to the phase
difference $\Delta\varphi$ between the echoes received in consecutive transmissions,
\begin{equation}
    \Delta\varphi = \frac{4\pi}{\lambda}\,\dot\rho\,T_c
    \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
    \dot\rho = \frac{\lambda\,\Delta\varphi}{4\pi\,T_c},
    \label{eq:doppler}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the carrier wavelength and $T_c$ is the interval between consecutive
transmissions.

As an active sensor, RaDAR operates independently of ambient lighting, both during the day and
at night.

Compared with LiDAR, RaDAR has lower angular resolution and therefore generally provides less
accurate position measurements. However, its signals have much longer wavelengths than the
laser light used by LiDAR and are less attenuated by dust, fog and rain, allowing more robust
operation in adverse weather conditions.

Within a sensor fusion framework, RaDAR complements LiDAR by providing range-rate measurements
and detecting targets beyond the range covered by LiDAR. Combining the two sensors can therefore improve the performance of the tracking system.

\subsection{Cameras}
\label{subsec:camera}

Cameras are passive sensors that capture images of the scene at high resolution and
frame rate. Their main strength is the semantic content of the images, since colour, texture
and shape make them well suited to the detection and classification of the surrounding objects.

Being passive, they depend on the ambient light, so that the quality of the images changes
with the brightness of the scene, as well as with the weather. Moreover, the scene is
projected onto the image plane and the depth is not measured directly, so that the accuracy
with which the distance of a target can be inferred degrades as the distance increases.

oriented at $\pm\SI{100}{\degree}$ with respect to the longitudinal axis, and two units on
the roll hoop facing forward and backward, so that together they cover the
surroundings of the vehicle in every direction. The Mako G319C is based on the Sony IMX265
global-shutter sensor, with a resolution of about 3.2 megapixels at up to \SI{37.6}{fps}.
Its main characteristics are reported in Table~\ref{tab:cam_av24}.

\section{Software Architecture}
\label{sec:sw_arch}

The autonomous driving software, shared by the two vehicles, is organised in four
main modules: estimation, perception, planning and control (Fig.~\ref{fig:sw_arch}).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \input{figures/ch2/tikz/sw_arch.tex}
    \caption{Main modules of the autonomous driving software and data exchanged between
    them.}
    \label{fig:sw_arch}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Estimation.} The estimation module reconstructs the current state of the ego
vehicle, i.e.\ its position, velocity and acceleration, by combining the measurements of
GNSS receivers, inertial units, LiDARs, RaDARs and wheel encoders with the feedback of
the actuators, i.e.\ braking, steering and throttle. The resulting description of the ego
state is then fed to the perception, planning and control modules.

\paragraph{Perception.}The perception module turns the raw data of LiDARs, RaDARs and cameras into a description of the vehicles around the ego car. For each sensor, the raw data are processed into a list of detections, each containing the position of a vehicle and, for the RaDAR, also its range rate. The detections of the different sensors are then passed to
the \emph{Target Tracking} module, which associates them over time with the vehicles
already known, fuses the information coming from the different sensors and estimates
the state of each opponent. The result is
the list of the opponents with their estimated state, which is the output of perception towards planning. The target
tracking module is the subject of this thesis and is described in detail in
Chapter~\ref{ch:tracking}.

\paragraph{Planning.} It receives the ego state from estimation, the opponent list
from perception and the data of a track database, which contains the
boundaries of the track and a set of reference trajectories. Based on the presence of
other vehicles and on the chosen strategy, i.e.\ following, overtaking or defending,
the planning module decides the trajectory that the vehicle has to follow. The output is a reference trajectory,
i.e.\ a path with the associated speed profile, which is sent to control.

\paragraph{Control.} It receives the reference trajectory from planning and the ego
state from estimation, and computes the commands for the actuators through two
controllers: a longitudinal controller, which acts on throttle, brake and gear to track
the reference speed, and a lateral controller, which computes the steering angle that
keeps the vehicle on the reference path.
