\chapter{Measurement Noise Analysis}
\label{ch:adaptive_r}

This chapter addresses the characterization of the measurement noise covariance matrix
$\mathbf{R}$ of the filter. As introduced in Section~\ref{sec:tt_correction}, each
measurement is modeled as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_k = h(\mathbf{x}_k) + \mathbf{v}_k,
    \qquad
    \mathbf{v}_k \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{0},\, \mathbf{R}_k\right),
    \label{eq:ch4_meas}
\end{equation}
where the measurement noise $\mathbf{v}_k$ represents the error of the sensor and of its
perception pipeline, and $\mathbf{R}_k$ describes how large this error is expected to be.
Through the covariance of the innovation $\mathbf{S}_k$, the matrix enters the Kalman
gain~\eqref{eq:tt_update} and sets how much each measurement corrects the prediction. A
correct tuning is therefore essential, since a covariance that is too large underuses the
information carried by the measurements, while one that is too small weights them too much
and makes the estimate noisy.

The chapter is divided into two parts. The first analyzes the measurement errors,
identifying and compensating their deterministic contributions, related to the reference,
to the calibration and to the working principle of each sensor, and then
characterizes the remaining error in the time and frequency domains. The second addresses
the tuning of $\mathbf{R}$, first with a static value representative of each sensor and
then with a covariance matching technique, which adapts it online from the innovations of
the filter. \\Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing} describes how the errors are computed and, the datasets used and their characteristics. The analysis of the errors is
developed in Sections~\ref{sec:deterministic}, \ref{sec:macro} and~\ref{sec:gauss_markov},
while Section~\ref{sec:meas_cov} presents the static and the adaptive measurement noise
covariance. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:ch4_future} outlines the future developments.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

Before the analysis of the characteristics of the measurement errors, the preliminary
sections explain how the errors are computed, i.e.\ how the reference of the opponent is
built and how the detections are associated with it to compute their mismatch, and present
the characteristics of the datasets.

\subsection{Ground Truth Reconstruction}
\label{sec:gt}

The reference for the state of the opponent is the state estimated by the opponent
vehicle itself. For each sample, the data contain the timestamp, the position and the heading of
the vehicle, its longitudinal speed and, when available, its longitudinal acceleration.
Since each team adopts its own format and reference system, the data are first brought
into a common structure, compatible with the logs of the ego vehicle. The position and
the heading of the opponent are roto-translated into the map frame of the ego vehicle.

When the longitudinal acceleration is measured by the inertial unit of the opponent, it is
low-pass filtered with a second-order zero-phase Butterworth filter. Otherwise, it is
obtained by differentiating the speed and smoothing the result with a Savitzky-Golay
filter. The yaw rate, when not provided, is obtained in the same way from the heading.

To compute the relative geometry of the two vehicles, each sample of the opponent is
associated with the sample of the ego vehicle with the closest timestamp. For each pair of
synchronized samples, the position of
the opponent in the frame of the ego vehicle is obtained by rotating the relative position
by the heading $\psi_e$ of the ego vehicle,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{rel}} =
    \begin{bmatrix} x_{\mathrm{rel}} \\ y_{\mathrm{rel}} \end{bmatrix}
    = \mathbf{R}(\psi_e)^\top \left(\mathbf{p}_o - \mathbf{p}_e\right),
    \qquad
    \psi_{\mathrm{rel}} = \psi_o - \psi_e,
    \label{eq:gt_rel}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{p}_o$, $\mathbf{p}_e$ and $\psi_o$, $\psi_e$ are the positions and the
headings of opponent and ego vehicle in the map frame, and $\mathbf{R}(\cdot)$ is the
rotation matrix defined in~\eqref{eq:tt_R_rot}. \\The range is computed as the distance between the
two vehicles, $\rho = \|\mathbf{p}_o - \mathbf{p}_e\|$, and the range rate is the
projection of the relative velocity on the line of sight,
\begin{equation}
    \dot\rho = \frac{\left(\mathbf{p}_o - \mathbf{p}_e\right)^\top
    \left(\mathbf{v}_o - \mathbf{v}_e\right)}{\rho},
    \qquad
    \mathbf{v}_i = v_i \begin{bmatrix} \cos\psi_i \\ \sin\psi_i \end{bmatrix},
    \quad i \in \{o,\,e\},
    \label{eq:gt_rhodot}
\end{equation}
with $v_o$ and $v_e$ the speeds of the two vehicles.

Finally, it is important to highlight that, the reference, is not free of errors. Since it coincides with the output of the
state estimation of the opponent, its quality depends on the performance of that
estimate, and, for example, a degradation of the GNSS measurements, as well as any other error of the
estimate, is directly inherited by the reference. Moreover, since each team adopts its own
estimation, the quality of the reference can differ from one opponent to another.

\subsection{Error Computation}
\label{subsec:err_frames}

Once the ground truth has been reconstructed, the latter is associated with the
measurements of the sensors in order to compute the errors. \\Since it is available at a higher frequency than the
detections, the ground truth is linearly interpolated at the acquisition time of each
detection, and the measurement error is defined as the difference between the position of the detection and the position of the
opponent given by the ground truth at the same instant.

The errors used in the following analysis are expressed in the frame of the ego vehicle,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{e} =
    \begin{bmatrix} e_x \\ e_y \end{bmatrix}
    = \mathbf{R}(\psi_e)^\top \left(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{det}} - \mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{gt}}\right),
    \label{eq:err_ego}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{det}}$ and $\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{gt}}$ are the positions of the
detection and of the ground truth in the map frame, and $\psi_e$ is the heading of the ego
vehicle, consistently with~\eqref{eq:gt_rel}. This frame is attached to the ego vehicle,
so its axes keep the same meaning along the whole lap, with $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$ always
positive ahead of the ego vehicle and $y_{\mathrm{rel}}$ positive to its left. As a
consequence, $e_x$ is always the error along the longitudinal direction of the ego vehicle
and $e_y$ the lateral one, regardless of the absolute orientation of the vehicles in the
map, and the errors collected along the lap can be compared and aggregated consistently.

\section{Reference and Calibration Errors}\label{sec:deterministic}

The first sources of errors to be identified are those related to the setup. \\As
will be shown in the following, two components are identified, one related to the calibration of
the sensors and one related to the reference. Being systematic and
repeatable, both can be identified and compensated. The longitudinal component is analyzed first, and then the lateral one.

\subsection*{Longitudinal Component}
\label{subsec:det_long}

The analysis starts from the longitudinal error of the LiDARs. Three sessions are
considered, 07/26\_TUM, 08/03\_Kinetiz and 10/14\_eva, and each of them shows a different
bias. Fig.~\ref{fig:det_long} shows the error of the two pipelines in a window of session
07/26\_TUM. In nominal operating conditions, i.e.\ with the opponent moving ahead of the
ego vehicle in the range 20--\SI{60}{m}, the mean error is \SI{0.41}{m} for Clustering and
\SI{0.36}{m} for PointPillars. In session 08/03\_Kinetiz it is close to zero, with
\SI{-0.03}{m} and \SI{-0.07}{m}, while in session 10/14\_eva, recorded at Yas Marina, it
is about \SI{0.2}{m} (Table~\ref{tab:det_long}). 
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch4/det_long_time.pdf}
    \caption[Longitudinal error of the LiDARs]{Longitudinal error of the LiDARs in a window of session 07/26\_TUM, with the mean
    in nominal conditions: \SI{0.41}{m} for Clustering and \SI{0.36}{m} for PointPillars.}
    \label{fig:det_long}
\end{figure}

A closer analysis of session 10/14\_eva suggests the origin of this bias. In this session
the opponent was a second vehicle operated by us, so both the perception of the ego vehicle
and the state estimation used as reference were known. Comparing the two, it emerged that
the perception returns the geometric center of the vehicle, while the estimation refers to
its center of mass. This difference, which is a matter of conventions and not of quality of
the perception, produces a constant longitudinal offset. Since different teams may refer
their state estimate to different points of the vehicle, the same cause can also explain
the different biases of the other two sessions.

The hypothesis is also supported by the behavior of the camera. In the range
20--\SI{60}{m}, where its error does not depend on the range (Fig.~\ref{fig:det_cam}), the
mean error of the camera is compatible with the one of the LiDARs, within its larger standard deviation (Table~\ref{tab:det_long}). The camera also measures the geometric center of
the vehicle, by reconstructing its bounding box, but through a different algorithm, and
finding the same bias further supports the idea that its origin lies in the reference.

For this reason, during the reconstruction of the ground truth a longitudinal offset is
added to the state estimate of the opponent, which is first estimated offline for each

\subsection*{Lateral Component}
\label{subsec:det_lat}

Passing to the lateral component, the lateral error of the LiDARs shows a clear
dependence on the relative position of the opponent. Fig.~\ref{fig:det_lat} shows it as a
function of the longitudinal position $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$ of the opponent, for session
07/26\_TUM. The error depends linearly on $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$. For this reason, the errors
are fitted with a straight line,
\begin{equation}
    e_y = a\,x_{\mathrm{rel}} + b,
    \label{eq:det_lat_fit}
\end{equation}
whose slope $a$ is estimated through a least squares fit on the data. The fit gives a
slope of \SI{0.60}{cm/m} for Clustering and \SI{0.58}{cm/m} for PointPillars. 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch4/det_lat_xrel.pdf}
    \caption[Lateral error of the LiDARs as a function of $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$]{Lateral error
    of the LiDARs as a function of $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$ in session 07/26\_TUM, with the least
    squares fit: slope of \SI{0.60}{cm/m} for Clustering and \SI{0.58}{cm/m} for
    PointPillars.}
    \label{fig:det_lat}
\end{figure}

The same analysis on session 08/03\_Kinetiz gives a slope of \SI{0.56}{cm/m} for
Clustering and \SI{0.52}{cm/m} for PointPillars, very close to the ones of the first
session, while the slope of the RaDAR is close to zero in both sessions and the one of the
camera changes from \SI{1.23}{cm/m} in session 07/26\_TUM to \SI{-0.26}{cm/m} in session
08/03\_Kinetiz. \\Since the trend is repeatable across the two datasets but observed only on
the LiDARs, its cause is related to these sensors rather than to the reference and it's compatible with a calibration offset of about \SI{0.33}{\degree} in yaw that produces a lateral error
$e_y \simeq \delta\psi\,x_{\mathrm{rel}}$.

Following the hypothesis formulated on the data recorded at Imola, a recalibration of the
three LiDARs was performed at Yas Marina, correcting their yaw by the identified offset.
As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:det_lat_yas}, the dependence of the lateral error on
$x_{\mathrm{rel}}$ is successfully removed.

\section{Macroscopic Error Trends}
\label{sec:macro}

Once the setup-related errors have been compensated, the errors related to the working
principle of each sensor and of its detection pipeline are analyzed. The analysis is
carried out on the first two moments of the errors, i.e.\ the mean and the standard
deviation, and looks for trends in them. It starts from the considerations of
Chapters~\ref{ch:setup} and~\ref{ch:tracking} on the working principle of the pipelines and
on the intrinsic characteristics of the sensors. For instance, having introduced how the
camera reconstructs the depth, the dependence of its longitudinal error on the range is
analyzed, while for the RaDAR, since one of its limits is the angular resolution, the
dependence of the standard deviation on the range is analyzed.


\subsection*{LiDAR}
\label{subsec:macro_lidar}

Starting with the LiDARs, Fig.~\ref{fig:lidar_clust_err} and Fig.~\ref{fig:lidar_pp_err}
show the errors of Clustering and PointPillars, together with their mean and standard
deviation, while the statistics of the sessions recorded at Yas Marina, on which the trends
identified at Imola are validated, are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:lidar}.

The errors of Clustering remain centered on zero, but their spread grows with the range,
above all on the lateral component. With the opponent ahead, the lateral standard
deviation in the sessions at Imola increases from 0.08--\SI{0.09}{m} at 10--\SI{20}{m} to
0.39--\SI{0.43}{m} at 90--\SI{100}{m}, while the longitudinal one grows less, from
0.05--\SI{0.18}{m} to 0.21--\SI{0.25}{m}. This behavior is related to the angular
resolution of the sensor, since the spacing between adjacent points grows linearly with the
range, so that the farther the opponent, the fewer and sparser the points on it, and the
less reliable the identification of the visible corner, on which the shift of the cluster to
the center of the vehicle relies (Section~\ref{sec:tt_perception}). The effect is larger on
the lateral component, which, with the opponent ahead, depends directly on the angular
position of the points.

With the opponent behind, the standard deviation reaches 0.30--\SI{0.69}{m} beyond
\SI{30}{m}. Here, the quality of the detections is limited by the blind spot caused by the
rear wing, which occludes the view of the LiDARs towards the back of the car
(Section~\ref{subsec:lidar}). At short range the opponent, which moves at a lateral offset
of about \SI{6}{m}, is still fully visible, but as it moves away it gets closer to the
longitudinal axis of the ego vehicle and progressively enters the blind spot. Only a
decreasing portion of the vehicle then remains visible, and the few points returned produce
a centroid of the cluster that changes from one scan to the next.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch4/lidar_clustering_errors.pdf}
    \caption{Longitudinal and lateral errors of LiDAR Clustering as a function of
    $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$, with mean and standard deviation in intervals of \SI{10}{m}.}
    \label{fig:lidar_clust_err}
\end{figure}

For PointPillars, the dependence on the range and on the occlusions manifests itself in a
different way. The standard deviation of its errors remains below \SI{0.2}{m}, with only a
slight growth of the lateral one with the range, consistent in all the sessions, since the
bounding box is reconstructed by a neural network that identifies the opponent consistently
even when it is farther or only partially visible. What degrades is instead the
reconstruction of the depth, because the returned points lie only on the surfaces of the
opponent facing the sensor and, the fewer they are, the more the bounding box collapses
towards them. With the opponent behind, the mean error is positive, between 0.13 and
\SI{0.30}{m} in the sessions at Imola, and it grows as the opponent moves away, while with
the opponent ahead it is close to zero at short range and becomes negative as the range
increases, reaching \SI{-0.19}{m} and \SI{-0.23}{m} at 90--\SI{100}{m}. Overall,
PointPillars therefore tends to produce detections shifted towards the ego vehicle.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch4/lidar_pointpillars_errors.pdf}
    \caption{Longitudinal and lateral errors of LiDAR PointPillars as a function of
    $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$, with mean and standard deviation in intervals of \SI{10}{m}.}
    \label{fig:lidar_pp_err}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{RaDAR}
\label{subsec:macro_radar}

Moving on to the RaDAR, Fig.~\ref{fig:radar_long} shows its longitudinal error as a function of the longitudinal
position of the opponent, separately for each session.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch4/radar_longitudinal_errors.pdf}
    \caption{Longitudinal error of the RaDAR as a function of $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$, with mean
    and standard deviation in intervals of \SI{10}{m}.}
    \label{fig:radar_long}
\end{figure}

Compared with the LiDARs, the RaDAR detections are much sparser. Already
at short range, the standard deviation of the longitudinal error is 0.42--\SI{0.52}{m},
three to four times the one of the LiDARs, and it tends to increase with the range,
although not monotonically, reaching 0.83--\SI{1.12}{m} behind and 0.71--\SI{0.82}{m}
ahead at 90--\SI{100}{m}.

The mean error differs with the opponent ahead and behind, which results in two different
longitudinal offsets, but in both cases it is directed towards the ego vehicle, with a trend
and a magnitude that depend on the session (Table~\ref{tab:rad_ex}). With the opponent
ahead, it is negative and tends to grow with the range, from \SI{-0.67}{m} and
\SI{-0.44}{m} at 10--\SI{20}{m} in sessions 07/26\_TUM and 08/03\_Kinetiz to \SI{-1.36}{m}
and \SI{-1.06}{m} at 90--\SI{100}{m}. With the opponent behind, it is positive and, on
average, smaller.

Both behaviors are explained by the working principle of the sensor. The standard
deviation is larger than the one of the LiDARs because the coarser angular resolution
samples the opponent with fewer points, while the mean error depends on the part of the
vehicle that is observed. With the opponent ahead, most of the radar waves are reflected by
the rear wing, a large surface at the rear end of the car and the closest to the sensor,
which shifts the detection towards the ego vehicle. With the opponent behind, the RaDAR
observes the nose, the front wing, the wheels and the sidepods, i.e.\ a reflecting surface
distributed along the car, and the offset is smaller.

Both the growth of the standard deviation and the two different offsets are validated on
the sessions recorded at Yas Marina, as reported in Appendix~\ref{app:lidar}.

\subsection*{Camera}
\label{subsec:macro_camera}

For the cameras only data with the opponent ahead are available. Fig.~\ref{fig:camera_long} shows the longitudinal error as a
function of the longitudinal position of the opponent, separately for each session.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch4/camera_longitudinal_errors.pdf}
    \caption{Longitudinal error of the camera as a function of $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$, with mean
    and standard deviation in intervals of \SI{10}{m}.}
    \label{fig:camera_long}
\end{figure}

The dependence of the camera on the range appears mainly in the
mean of the longitudinal error, which follows a U-shaped trend. At short range, below
\SI{20}{m}, the detection is farther than the ground truth by about \SI{0.6}{m}. Between
20 and \SI{60}{m}, i.e.\ in the operating range identified in
Section~\ref{subsec:det_long}, the mean error is close to zero, within
$\pm\SI{0.1}{m}$, and the bias of the camera is comparable to the one of the LiDARs. Beyond this range, it increases again and reaches
0.25--\SI{0.40}{m} at 90--\SI{100}{m}, with a dependence that, from \SI{40}{m} on, is
linear. It is quantified by the slope $m$ of the least squares line fitted to the $N$
detections in a range interval $x_a \le x_{\mathrm{rel}} \le x_b$,
\begin{equation}
    e_x = m\,x_{\mathrm{rel}} + q,
    \qquad
    m = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(x_{\mathrm{rel},i} - \bar{x}_{\mathrm{rel}}\right)
    \left(e_{x,i} - \bar{e}_x\right)}
    {\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(x_{\mathrm{rel},i} - \bar{x}_{\mathrm{rel}}\right)^2},
    \label{eq:macro_slope}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{x}_{\mathrm{rel}}$ and $\bar{e}_x$ are the means over the interval. Between 40
and \SI{100}{m}, the slope is \SI{0.92}{cm/m} in session 07/26\_TUM and
\SI{0.81}{cm/m} in session 08/03\_Kinetiz. The standard deviation
remains between 0.2 and \SI{0.5}{m}, without a clear trend with the range.

The growth of the error with the range is related to the way the camera estimates the
depth, which is obtained from the size of the bounding box in the image
(Section~\ref{sec:tt_perception}). As the opponent moves away, it spans fewer pixels and
its bounding box is estimated too small, so that the opponent is placed farther than it
actually is. The same trend is observed in the sessions recorded at Yas Marina, with slopes
between \SI{0.70}{cm/m} and \SI{1.49}{cm/m} (Appendix~\ref{app:lidar}), so that it can be
compensated with the linear model~\eqref{eq:macro_slope}. Alternatively, the dependence on
the range can be corrected by using as a prior the depth provided by another sensor, such as the LiDAR.

\section{Time-Frequency Residual Analysis}
\label{sec:gauss_markov}

Starting from the measurement errors, once the trends identified in the previous section
have been removed offline, an analysis in the time and frequency domains is carried out. The
residual errors of the LiDARs and of the camera show a slow, low-frequency component, on
which a white, high-frequency one is superimposed. This makes the residuals autocorrelated,
which
breaks the assumption of the Kalman filter on the measurement errors, namely that they are
white and uncorrelated between different time instants,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{v}_k\,\mathbf{v}_j^\top\right] = \mathbf{R}_k\,\delta_{kj}.
    \label{eq:white_noise}
\end{equation}
The idea is therefore to characterize this behavior, in order to compensate it.

\subsection*{Spectral Analysis}
\label{subsec:acf_psd}

First of all, the low-frequency content of the errors is quantified through the power
spectral density (PSD) of the residual errors, which describes how their power is
distributed over the frequencies. The PSD $S_e(f)$ is estimated with the Welch method,
which divides the time series into $K$ continuous segments of $N$ samples $e_{i,n}$ and
computes on each of them, weighted by a window $w_n$, the periodogram, i.e.\ the squared
magnitude of the discrete Fourier transform,
\begin{equation}
    P_i(f) = \frac{\Delta t}{N\,U}
    \left|\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} w_n\, e_{i,n}\, e^{-j 2\pi f n \Delta t}\right|^2,
    \qquad
    U = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} w_n^2,
    \label{eq:periodogram}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta t$ the sampling period. To obtain a more robust estimator, the
PSD is obtained by averaging the periodograms of the segments
\begin{equation}
    S_e(f) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{i=1}^{K} P_i(f).
    \label{eq:welch}
\end{equation}
A Hann window of $N = 128$ samples is adopted. Since each segment requires consecutive
detections without interruptions, longer segments, of 256 or 512 samples, would reduce the
number of segments available for the average. For the same reason, consecutive windows
overlap by \SI{50}{\percent}, which increases the number of periodograms to be averaged.

For sessions 07/26\_TUM and 10/14\_eva with the opponent ahead, the PSD of the residual
errors is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:psd} and summarized in Table~\ref{tab:psd_sessions}.
The LiDARs and the camera have the largest low-frequency contribution, with
44--\SI{80}{\percent} of the power below \SI{1}{Hz} and 62--\SI{91}{\percent} below
\SI{2}{Hz}, so that their error is dominated by low frequencies. The RaDAR has on average
the smallest, with 45--\SI{64}{\percent} of the power below \SI{1}{Hz}.

\subsection*{Geometric Dependence}
\label{subsec:corr_geometry}

To compensate this behavior, a model that explains the autocorrelation of the residual
errors is sought. The analysis focuses on the relative geometry of the two vehicles, not
only because the dependence of the errors on the direction from which the opponent is
observed has not been analyzed yet, but also because a hint comes from the previous
section, where the longitudinal errors of the RaDAR and of PointPillars showed that the
detection is shifted according to the surface of the vehicle visible to the sensor. The
idea is therefore that the face of the opponent that is seen can influence the detection in
the same way and, since it changes slowly along the lap, be one of the origins of the
autocorrelation. The relative geometry is described by two quantities.
The bearing
\begin{equation}
    \alpha = \operatorname{atan2}\left(y_{\mathrm{rel}},\, x_{\mathrm{rel}}\right)
    \label{eq:geo_bearing}
\end{equation}
is the direction of the line of sight towards the opponent in the frame of the ego vehicle,
i.e.\ where the opponent is seen from the ego vehicle. The aspect angle
\begin{equation}
    \varphi = \alpha - \psi_{\mathrm{rel}}
    \label{eq:geo_aspect}
\end{equation}
is the same line of sight expressed relative to the heading of the opponent, with
$\psi_{\mathrm{rel}}$ the relative yaw of~\eqref{eq:gt_rel}, i.e.\ which side of the opponent
is seen by the sensors.

The analysis is carried out on the residual errors of sessions 07/26\_TUM and 10/14\_eva with
the opponent ahead, i.e.\ on the same data as Tables~\ref{tab:psd_sessions}
and~\ref{tab:acf_sessions}. For each sensor and component, the dependence of the residual error
$e_k$ on each of them, $g_k \in \{\alpha_k, \varphi_k\}$, is measured with the correlation
coefficient
\begin{equation}
    r = \frac{\sum_{k}\left(e_k - \bar{e}\right)\left(g_k - \bar{g}\right)}
    {\sqrt{\sum_{k}\left(e_k - \bar{e}\right)^2}\,\sqrt{\sum_{k}\left(g_k - \bar{g}\right)^2}},
    \label{eq:geo_corr}
\end{equation}
computed on all the detections of the session. The results are reported in
Table~\ref{tab:geo_dependence}.

\subsection*{Gauss-Markov Bias Model}
\label{subsec:gauss_markov_model}

Having excluded a deterministic origin, the error of each sensor and component is
modeled as the sum of two stochastic processes,
\begin{equation}
    e_k = b_k + w_k,
    \qquad
    w_k \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\, \sigma_w^2\right),
    \label{eq:gm_decomposition}
\end{equation}
where $b_k$ is the low-frequency component, here referred to as bias, and $w_k$ the white,
high-frequency one. Since the two components are independent, the variance of the error is
$\sigma_b^2 + \sigma_w^2$. The idea is to find a model of $b_k$, in order to isolate $w_k$ by
difference. As a preliminary step towards an online implementation, the model is first
defined and tested offline on the residual errors.

The choice of the model is guided by the analysis above. Since the errors are strongly
autocorrelated, the bias is modeled as a first-order Gauss-Markov process,
\begin{equation}
    b_{k+1} = \phi\, b_k + \eta_k,
    \qquad
    \phi = e^{-\Delta t/\tau},
    \qquad
    \eta_k \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\, \sigma_b^2\,(1-\phi^2)\right),
    \label{eq:gm_process}
\end{equation}
with $\Delta t$ the sampling period and $\tau$ the correlation time, i.e.\ at each step the
bias decays exponentially and is perturbed by the white noise $\eta_k$. Its autocorrelation decays as
$\rho_b(\ell) = e^{-|\ell|/\tau}$, so that, by choosing $\tau$ consistently with the
autocorrelation of the residuals, the bias can be reconstructed.

The correlation time is chosen from the values of Table~\ref{tab:acf_sessions}, and a value
of $\tau = \SI{1}{s}$ is adopted for all the sensors, for simplicity. It does not represent
the optimal value for each sensor, but it proves to be an effective choice. \\ Indeed, when the model
is applied offline to the detrended errors and the estimated bias is removed, the
residuals become white, with a normalized autocorrelation of about zero at all the lags,
as reported in Table~\ref{tab:acf_gm}.

Finally, once the errors are decorrelated, the standard deviations of the white component
and of the low-frequency bias can be computed. Since $w_k$ is uncorrelated between consecutive
samples, the covariance of two consecutive errors depends only on $b_k$.
Following~\eqref{eq:gm_process}, $\sigma_b^2$ is estimated by least squares on the pairs
of consecutive detections, and $\sigma_w^2$ is obtained by difference,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[e_k\, e_{k+1}\right] = \phi_k\, \sigma_b^2
    \;\Rightarrow\;
    \hat{\sigma}_b^2 = \frac{\sum_k \phi_k\, e_k\, e_{k+1}}{\sum_k \phi_k^2},
    \qquad
    \hat{\sigma}_w^2 = \mathrm{Var}(e) - \hat{\sigma}_b^2,
    \label{eq:gm_sigmas}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_k$ is computed with the actual time between the two detections.

The results in Table~\ref{tab:gm_sigmas} confirm what emerged from the analysis of the PSD
and of the autocorrelation. For the LiDARs and the camera, a considerable part of the
standard deviation of the error is introduced by the bias. For the RaDAR, instead, the
bias is still present, but it is less relevant than the white component.

To estimate the bias online, the natural approach, as prescribed by the literature, is to
augment the state of the filter with the bias of each sensor, which evolves as in~\eqref{eq:gm_process}, so that the filter estimates the
position of the opponent and the biases at the same time,
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\mathbf{x}} =
    \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{x} \\ \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{L}} \\ \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{C}} \\
    \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{R}} \end{bmatrix},
    \qquad
    \mathbf{z}_s = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{b}_s + \mathbf{w}_s,
    \qquad
    \mathbf{R}_s = \sigma_{w,s}^2\,\mathbf{I},
    \label{eq:gm_augmented}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{H}\mathbf{x}$ is the position of the opponent and the
subscripts L, C and R denote the LiDARs, the camera and the RaDAR. Each measurement is
then the sum of the position of the opponent, of the bias of the sensor and of a white
noise.

Implementing this approach first on the LiDARs, the results show that their bias is not
observable. In the prediction step, the position of the opponent and the bias are
propagated through their own models, i.e.\ the motion model~\eqref{eq:tt_system} and the
Gauss-Markov process~\eqref{eq:gm_process}, respectively, while a LiDAR measurement
observes their sum, so that the correction step has to split a single innovation between
the two states. Considering a single component, with position $p$ and bias $b$, the
measurement matrix of the LiDARs is $\mathbf{H} = [\,1 \;\; 1\,]$ and, following the
correction step of Section~\ref{sec:tt_correction}, the innovation
$\nu = z - (\hat{p} + \hat{b})$ has variance
\begin{equation}
    S = P_{pp} + P_{bb} + 2P_{pb} + R,
    \label{eq:gm_innov_var}
\end{equation}
and the Kalman gains of the two states are
\begin{equation}
    K_p = \frac{P_{pp} + P_{pb}}{S},
    \qquad
    K_b = \frac{P_{pb} + P_{bb}}{S},
    \label{eq:gm_gains}
\end{equation}
where $P_{pp}$ and $P_{bb}$ are the variances of the predicted position and of the
predicted bias, and $P_{pb}$ is their cross-covariance. The uncertainty of the position is always larger than the one
of the bias, because the motion model has to account for possible changes in the dynamics
of the opponent, whereas the bias evolves slowly, driven by the small process noise
of~\eqref{eq:gm_process}. Hence $P_{pp} \gg P_{bb}$ and $K_p \gg K_b$, so that most of the
correction is assigned to the position. In practice, a variation of the bias is
interpreted as a displacement of the opponent, and the tracker follows the bias instead of
estimating it.

Separating the bias from the motion would require an independent sensor, not affected by
the bias of the LiDARs, for which $\mathbf{H} = [\,1 \;\; 0\,]$ and the bias is updated only
through the cross-covariance, with gain $K_b = P_{pb}/S$. In the setup considered, however,
no other sensor provides position measurements as accurate as those of the LiDARs, so this
is not possible. For this reason, the slow component of the error is not removed online,
and it is instead taken into account in the tuning of the measurement noise covariance,
presented in Section~\ref{sec:meas_cov}. The biases of the camera and of the RaDAR, on the
other hand, remain observable relative to the LiDARs, since the difference
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}_s - \mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{L}} =
    \mathbf{b}_s - \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{L}} + \mathbf{w}_s - \mathbf{w}_{\mathrm{L}},
    \qquad s \in \{\mathrm{C}, \mathrm{R}\},
    \label{eq:gm_relative}
\end{equation}
does not depend on the position of the opponent. The estimation of these relative biases
is left as future work, rather than implemented in this work, as explained in
Section~\ref{sec:ch4_future}.

\section{Measurement Noise Covariance Tuning}
\label{sec:meas_cov}

As a concluding part of the chapter, the tuning of the measurement noise covariance matrix
is presented. A static value is proposed first. Then, to handle the variability of the
standard deviation, due either to the range, as shown in Section~\ref{sec:macro}, or to
local conditions, a covariance matching technique is introduced.

\subsection{Gaussian Fit Tuning}
\label{sec:gaussian_fit}

The static value of the measurement noise covariance is set from the overall residual
errors of Section~\ref{sec:gauss_markov}, which include both the slow and the white
components. The uncertainty of each sensor is expressed by fitting a Gaussian distribution
to the residual position errors, separately for the longitudinal and the lateral
components, which correspond to $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_y$ of the measurement noise
model~\eqref{eq:tt_R_rot}. The parameter of the distribution is estimated as the sample
standard deviation of the residuals,
\begin{equation}
    \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(r_i - \bar{r}\right)^2},
    \label{eq:sigma_std}
\end{equation}
where $r_i$ are the $N$ residuals and $\bar{r}$ their mean.

Table~\ref{tab:sensor_noise} reports the resulting values. Besides those of the single
sessions, which differ in the number of detections and in the local conditions and can
therefore be slightly different, all the data are gathered to obtain a general statistic,
reported in the rightmost column. The results confirm the behavior that emerges from the
previous sections. The LiDARs are the most precise sensors on both components and, when
available, they are the main source for the correction of the predicted position of the
opponent. Between the two pipelines, Clustering shows the larger uncertainty, since its
spread grows with the range and when the opponent is in the blind spot
(Section~\ref{subsec:macro_lidar}). The camera follows, still providing a useful position
measurement, although its mean error depends strongly on the range
(Section~\ref{subsec:macro_camera}). The RaDAR shows the largest variability of the
position measurement, mainly on the longitudinal component, so that its position is useful
when the other sensors cannot detect the opponent, i.e.\ at long range with the opponent
behind, while its main contribution remains the direct measurement of the range rate.

The distribution of the errors of all the sessions, together with the fitted Gaussian

dominated by the tails, and the fitted Gaussian distribution is wider than the core. A
fixed value of $\mathbf{R}$ therefore underestimates the accuracy of most of the

motivates the adaptive approach of Section~\ref{sec:adaptive_r}.

accuracy declared in the datasheets, which refers to optimal conditions, and they would
change with a dataset of different characteristics, e.g.\ the standard deviation of the
RaDAR and of Clustering, which grows with the range, would be larger with the median range
shifted to \SI{90}{m}. Since the purpose of the analysis is to weight each sensor correctly
in the filter, these are nevertheless the appropriate values, as they reflect the errors
expected in the operating conditions of the vehicle.

\subsection{Covariance Matching}
\label{sec:adaptive_r}

In light of the previous sections, which highlighted the limits of a static value of
$\mathbf{R}$, a covariance matching technique is implemented. The method belongs to the
family of innovation-based adaptive estimation, and it estimates $\mathbf{R}$ online by
matching the sample covariance of the innovations of the filter with the one predicted by
the filter itself. In this way, $\mathbf{R}$ follows the actual quality of the
measurement, increasing when the innovations are larger than predicted and decreasing when
they are smaller.

For a Kalman filter, the covariance of the innovations $\boldsymbol{\nu}_k$ is
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{S}_k = \mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\nu}_k\,\boldsymbol{\nu}_k^\top\right]
    = \mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}\,\mathbf{H}^\top + \mathbf{R}_k.
    \label{eq:cm_innov_cov}
\end{equation}
For a consistent filter, the covariance of the measurement errors is therefore obtained
from the one of the innovations, minus the share due to the uncertainty of the filter,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{R}_k = \mathbf{S}_k - \mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}\,\mathbf{H}^\top.
    \label{eq:cm_R}
\end{equation}
With large innovations and a small $\mathbf{P}$, $\mathbf{R}$ increases, since the
measurement is worse than assumed, while with small innovations and a small $\mathbf{P}$,
$\mathbf{R}$ is small. The formulation, however, does not exclude the case in which
$\mathbf{R}$ becomes negative definite, when the innovations are smaller than predicted by
the uncertainty of the filter. For this reason, a lower bound is imposed on the diagonal
elements of $\mathbf{R}$.

The fundamental assumption of the method is that the innovations are a zero-mean, white
sequence, uncorrelated between different time instants. The assumption is necessary
because only in this case each innovation is representative of the error of the
measurement at that instant, so that, subtracting the share due to the uncertainty of the
filter, the error of the sensor is correctly isolated. The covariance $\mathbf{S}_k$ is
then estimated from the products $\boldsymbol{\nu}_k\,\boldsymbol{\nu}_k^\top$ of the
innovations, and the way the past innovations are combined is a design choice. The estimate
can be computed as a moving average over the last $M$ innovations,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{R}}_k = \frac{1}{M}\sum_{j=k-M+1}^{k}
    \left(\boldsymbol{\nu}_j\,\boldsymbol{\nu}_j^\top
    - \mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{P}_{j|j-1}\,\mathbf{H}^\top\right),
    \label{eq:cm_R_window}
\end{equation}
or, as done in this work, recursively, retaining the information of the past innovations
through a mixing coefficient $\alpha \in (0,1)$,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{R}}_k = \alpha\,\hat{\mathbf{R}}_{k-1}
    + (1-\alpha)\left(\boldsymbol{\nu}_k\,\boldsymbol{\nu}_k^\top
    - \mathbf{H}\,\mathbf{P}_{k|k-1}\,\mathbf{H}^\top\right).
    \label{eq:cm_R_alpha}
\end{equation}
A larger value of $\alpha$ keeps $\hat{\mathbf{R}}_k$ close to its value at the previous
step, while a smaller one updates it more rapidly with the new information. The value of
$\alpha$ is chosen so that $\hat{\mathbf{R}}_k$ follows the trend of the measurement errors
while remaining robust to spurious outliers. The recursion is initialized with the nominal
value of Section~\ref{sec:gaussian_fit}.

\subsection{Validation of the Adaptive Covariance}
\label{sec:adaptive_r_results}

The covariance matching is implemented online in the filter following~\eqref{eq:cm_R_alpha},
with a lower bound on the diagonal elements of $\mathbf{R}$ corresponding to a standard
deviation of \SI{0.03}{m} and a mixing coefficient $\alpha = 0.95$, and the adaptive
covariance $\hat{\mathbf{R}}_k$ is used in the correction step in place of the nominal
value. At each step, $\hat{\mathbf{R}}_k$ is also logged, so that it can be analyzed offline
together with the measurement errors.

The validation is carried out on the errors whitened offline with the Gauss-Markov model of
Section~\ref{subsec:gauss_markov_model}, i.e.\ on the residual errors from which the slow
component has been removed, and the adaptive covariance is compared with two Gaussian
distributions. The first has the nominal standard deviation of
Section~\ref{sec:gaussian_fit}, while the second has the standard deviation $\sigma_w$ of
the white component, estimated with~\eqref{eq:gm_sigmas}. The latter is the fairer
comparison, since the nominal value is tuned on the residual errors and therefore also
contains the variance $\sigma_b^2$ of the bias.

Since $\hat{\mathbf{R}}_k$ is adapted at each instant, it describes at each instant a
different zero-mean Gaussian distribution of the measurement error. To compare it with the
other two, its distribution over the whole session is obtained as the average of these
Gaussian distributions,
\begin{equation}
    \bar{p}(e_i) = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathcal{N}\!\left(e_i;\, 0,\, \hat{R}_{k,i}\right),
    \qquad i \in \{x,\,y\},
    \label{eq:cm_avg_density}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{R}_{k,i}$ is the diagonal element of $\hat{\mathbf{R}}_k$ of the component $i$,
longitudinal or lateral, and $K$ the number of measurements.

Fig.~\ref{fig:cm_clust} shows the comparison for LiDAR Clustering, whose whitened errors
are not Gaussian distributed, with most of them concentrated in a narrow central core
around zero and a smaller number, related to the instants in which the quality of the
measurement degrades, spread over much wider tails. The nominal distribution is oversized,
since its variance also includes the variance $\sigma_b^2$ of the bias, while the
distribution of $\sigma_w$, which describes only the white component, approximates the
errors better but, being a single Gaussian distribution, has to widen to follow the tails
and cannot reconstruct the narrow core. The average adaptive
distribution~\eqref{eq:cm_avg_density}, on the contrary, is a mixture of zero-mean Gaussian
distributions with different variances, so that it reproduces the narrow core through the
instants in which the error is small and the heavy tails through those in which it is
large.

Overall, the adaptive covariance is more consistent than the nominal value, and it
reconstructs the distribution of the measurement error from the innovations and from the
covariance of the filter. Being estimated from the innovations, however, it describes only
the white component of the error and is blind to the slow one, which is not observable.
Used in the correction step, it therefore weights the measurements according to their
instantaneous dispersion, while the slow component remains in the estimate
(Section~\ref{subsec:gauss_markov_model}).

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:ch4_future}

In this chapter, the covariance matching has been applied only to the LiDARs, which are the
most accurate sensors and the main source for the correction of the position of the
opponent. For these sensors, the adaptive covariance proves to be consistent even though
their low-frequency bias is not observable online. The reason is that the filter follows the
bias, so that the bias does not appear in the innovations, and the adaptive approach only
sees the white, high-frequency component of the error, which is exactly the one it is meant
to describe.

A natural extension of this work would be to apply the same approach also to the camera and
to the RaDAR. For these two sensors, however, the situation is different. Their innovations
are computed with respect to an estimate that follows the bias $\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{L}}$ of
the LiDARs, and they therefore contain not only the white component of their own error, but
also the difference between their bias and the one of the LiDARs,
\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol{\nu}_s \simeq \left(\mathbf{b}_s - \mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{L}}\right) + \mathbf{w}_s,
    \qquad s \in \{\mathrm{C}, \mathrm{R}\}.
    \label{eq:fw_innov}
\end{equation}
Since this relative bias varies slowly and can be as large as the white component, as for
the offsets of the RaDAR or the range-dependent trend of the camera seen in
Section~\ref{sec:macro}, an adaptive covariance computed from these innovations would follow
the relative bias and would not reflect the actual accuracy of the sensor.

To apply the covariance matching to the camera and to the RaDAR, the relative bias should
therefore be estimated first. This is possible because, unlike the absolute bias, the
relative one is observable, as shown in~\eqref{eq:gm_relative}, and it could be added to the
state of the filter as a Gauss-Markov process, similarly to what was proposed for the LiDARs
in Section~\ref{subsec:gauss_markov_model}. Once the estimated relative bias is subtracted
from the measurements, the innovations would contain only the white component of the error,
and the covariance matching could be applied to them in the same way as for the LiDARs.

This extension is left as future work because its benefit would be limited. It would provide
consistent innovations for the camera and the RaDAR and, as a consequence, a better weighting
of their measurements in the filter. However, the estimate of the state would still be
referred to the LiDARs and would therefore remain affected by their bias, which cannot be
compensated with the sensors currently available.
