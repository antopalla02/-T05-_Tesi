\chapter{Measurement Noise Analysis}
\label{ch:adaptive_r}

In this chapter,  an in depth analysis and characterization of the measurement noise of the sensors introduced in
Chapter~\ref{ch:tracking} is presented. The latter is of particular relevance mainly because the covariance of the measurement noise regulates the correction step of the Kalman
filter and therefore the quality of the estimate; indeed, a covariance that is too large
under uses the information carried by the measurements, while one that is too small
weights them too much making the estimate too noisy.

The chapter is divided into two parts. The first part aims at characterizing the
measurement noise of the sensors. The errors of each source are analyzed, and their
deterministic components, are identified and compensated: in this way,
only the random part of the error has to be described by the measurement noise
covariance, and each measurement is weighted correctly by the filter.

The second part introduces an adaptive approach to model the fact that the measurement
errors depend on the conditions in which the measurements are taken, e.g.\ a partial
occlusion of the opponent or a degraded detection quality, so that the measurement noise
covariance is adapted to these conditions.

Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing} describes the pre-processing of the data, i.e.\ the
reconstruction of the ground truth and the reference frames of the errors, and
Section~\ref{sec:datasets} analyzes the datasets. Sections~\ref{sec:deterministic},
\ref{sec:macro} and~\ref{sec:gauss_markov} address, respectively, the deterministic
components of the errors, their macroscopic correlations and their temporal structure.
Section~\ref{sec:adaptive_r} presents the adaptive approach and
Section~\ref{sec:ch4_results} the results.

\section{Pre-processing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}

The analysis of the measurement errors requires each detection to be compared with the
true position of the opponent at the instant in which it was acquired. This section
describes how the reference trajectory of the opponent is obtained and synchronized with
the detections, how the detections are associated with it, and in which reference frames
the resulting errors are expressed.

\subsection{Ground Truth Reconstruction}
\label{sec:gt}

The reference for the state of the opponent is the state estimated by the opponent
vehicle itself. During the sessions, each team records the output of its own state
estimation, based on RTK-GNSS receivers and inertial units, and shares it after the
session. For each sample, the data contain the timestamp, the position and the heading of
the vehicle, its longitudinal speed and, when available, its longitudinal acceleration.
Since each team adopts its own format and reference system, the data are first brought
into a common structure, compatible with the logs of the ego vehicle. The position and
the heading of the opponent are roto-translated into the map frame of the ego vehicle, so that the two vehicles are
described with respect to the same origin and orientation. When the estimate of the
opponent refers to a point different from the one adopted for the ego vehicle, the
position is also shifted along the longitudinal axis of the vehicle, so that both
refer to the same point.

When the longitudinal acceleration is measured by the
inertial unit of the opponent, it is low-pass filtered with a zero-phase Butterworth
filter, to remove the high-frequency content due to vibrations; otherwise, it is
obtained by differentiating the speed and smoothing the result with a Savitzky-Golay
filter. The yaw rate, when not provided, is obtained in the same way from the heading.

To compute the relative geometry of the two vehicles, each sample of the opponent is
associated with the sample of the ego vehicle with the closest timestamp. For each pair of
synchronized samples, the position of
the opponent in the frame of the ego vehicle is obtained by rotating the relative position
by the heading $\psi_e$ of the ego vehicle,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{rel}} =
    \begin{bmatrix} x_{\mathrm{rel}} \\ y_{\mathrm{rel}} \end{bmatrix}
    = \mathbf{R}(\psi_e)^\top \left(\mathbf{p}_o - \mathbf{p}_e\right),
    \qquad
    \psi_{\mathrm{rel}} = \psi_o - \psi_e,
    \label{eq:gt_rel}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{p}_o$, $\mathbf{p}_e$ and $\psi_o$, $\psi_e$ are the positions and the
headings of opponent and ego vehicle in the map frame, and $\mathbf{R}(\cdot)$ is the
rotation matrix defined in~\eqref{eq:tt_R_rot}. The range is the distance between the
two vehicles, $\rho = \|\mathbf{p}_o - \mathbf{p}_e\|$, and the range rate is the
projection of the relative velocity on the line of sight,
\begin{equation}
    \dot\rho = \frac{\left(\mathbf{p}_o - \mathbf{p}_e\right)^\top
    \left(\mathbf{v}_o - \mathbf{v}_e\right)}{\rho},
    \qquad
    \mathbf{v}_i = v_i \begin{bmatrix} \cos\psi_i \\ \sin\psi_i \end{bmatrix},
    \quad i \in \{o,\,e\},
    \label{eq:gt_rhodot}
\end{equation}
with $v_o$ and $v_e$ the speeds of the two vehicles.

It is important to note that the reference obtained in this way is not an exact ground truth. As stated at the beginning of the section, it coincides with the output of the state estimation pipeline of the opponent, and its quality is therefore strictly related to the performance of that pipeline: a degradation of the GNSS measurements, as well as any other error of the estimate, is directly inherited by the reference. Moreover, since each opponent adopts its own pipeline, the quality of the reference can differ from one opponent to another.

\subsection{Reference Frames of the Errors}
\label{subsec:err_frames}

The first step to compute the measurement errors is the association between the ground
truth and the measurements of the sensors. Since the ground truth is available at
\SI{100}{Hz}, while the detections are produced at 10--\SI{16.7}{Hz}, the ground truth is
linearly interpolated at the acquisition time of each detection. The measurement error is
then the difference between the position of the detection and the position of the
opponent given by the ground truth at the same instant.

The errors are expressed in the frame of the ego vehicle,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{e} =
    \begin{bmatrix} e_x \\ e_y \end{bmatrix}
    = \mathbf{R}(\psi_e)^\top \left(\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{det}} - \mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{gt}}\right),
    \label{eq:err_ego}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{det}}$ and $\mathbf{p}_{\mathrm{gt}}$ are the positions of the
detection and of the ground truth in the map frame, and $\psi_e$ is the heading of the ego
vehicle, consistently with~\eqref{eq:gt_rel}. This frame is attached to the ego vehicle,
so its axes keep the same meaning along the whole lap: $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$ is always
positive ahead of the ego vehicle and $y_{\mathrm{rel}}$ positive to its left. As a
consequence, $e_x$ is always the error along the longitudinal direction of the ego vehicle
and $e_y$ the lateral one, regardless of the absolute orientation of the vehicles in the
map, and the errors collected along the lap can be compared and aggregated consistently.

\section{Dataset Analysis}
\label{sec:datasets}

This section provides an in-depth analysis of the datasets used to develop the
characterization of the sensors. The two sessions, recorded on 26 July 2026 and on
3 August 2026 and referred to in the following as session 07/26\_TUM and session 08/03\_Kinetiz, both
took place at the Autodromo Internazionale Enzo e Dino Ferrari in Imola, with two
different opponents, TUM and Kinetiz respectively. Their main characteristics are
summarized in Table~\ref{tab:sessions}.

\begin{table}[H]
    \centering
    \small
    \begin{tabular}{lcc}
        \toprule
                                             & Session 07/26\_TUM            & Session 08/03\_Kinetiz            \\
        \midrule
        Date                                 & 26/07/2026              & 03/08/2026              \\
        Opponent                             & TUM                     & Kinetiz                 \\
        Duration                             & \SI{1261}{s}            & \SI{1780}{s}            \\
        Distance covered by the opponent     & \SI{29.2}{km} (6 laps)  & \SI{43.3}{km} (9 laps)  \\
        Median / maximum speed of the opponent & \SI{90}{} / \SI{163}{km/h} & \SI{91}{} / \SI{156}{km/h} \\
        Opponent stationary                  & \SI{139}{s}             & \SI{74}{s}              \\
        Opponent ahead of the ego vehicle    & \SI{72}{\percent}       & \SI{35}{\percent}       \\
        Position swaps                       & 1                       & 7                       \\
        Median range                         & \SI{48.5}{m}            & \SI{49.2}{m}            \\
        Median lateral position $y_{\mathrm{rel}}$ & \SI{6.0}{m}       & \SI{-5.7}{m}            \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    \caption{Main characteristics of the two sessions. Range and lateral position are
    computed while the opponent is detected by at least one sensor; the lateral position
    is positive to the left of the ego vehicle.}
    \label{tab:sessions}
\end{table}

Both sessions cover a wide range of dynamic conditions. The opponent travels at a median
speed of about \SI{90}{km/h} and reaches more than \SI{150}{km/h}, alternating braking,
cornering and traction phases along the lap. The two sessions also include several safe
stops, during which the opponent is stationary for \SI{139}{s} and \SI{74}{s}
respectively: since the sensors keep detecting it, these intervals allow the errors to be
characterized also in static conditions. Session 08/03\_Kinetiz contains seven position swaps
between the two vehicles at close range, i.e.\ overtakes. Overall, the combination of
safe stops, overtakes and laps at high speed ensures that the whole spectrum of the
dynamics of interest is covered by the datasets.

The two sessions are also complementary in the relative geometry between the two
vehicles. In session 07/26\_TUM the opponent is ahead of the ego vehicle for most of the time
and moves mainly on its left, both when ahead and when behind, with a median lateral
position of about \SI{6}{m}. In session 08/03\_Kinetiz the ego vehicle leads for most of the time,
and the opponent moves mainly on its right, with a median lateral position of about
\SI{-6}{m}. The two datasets therefore observe the opponent from symmetric points of view.

The availability of the detections depends on the position of the opponent with respect
to the ego vehicle. Fig.~\ref{fig:ds_rate} shows the detection rate of each sensor as a
function of the range, separately for the opponent ahead of and behind the ego vehicle;
the rate is computed as the number of detections associated with the opponent in each
range interval divided by the time spent by the opponent in that interval, merging the
two sessions. With the opponent ahead, both LiDAR pipelines detect it at close to the
nominal rate of \SI{10}{Hz} up to about \SI{50}{m}, and the rate decreases gradually with
the range, down to about \SI{100}{m} for both clustering and PointPillars.
With the opponent behind, instead, the rate drops already beyond \SI{30}{m} and becomes
negligible beyond 50--\SI{60}{m}: this is the effect of the blind spot
caused by the rear wing (Section~\ref{subsec:lidar}), which leaves only a few points on
the opponent. The RaDAR does not suffer from this limitation and keeps detecting the
opponent behind up to \SI{150}{m}, becoming the main source of information when the opponent is trailing.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch4/detection_rate_range.pdf}
    \caption{Detection rate as a function of the range, with the opponent ahead and behind.}
    \label{fig:ds_rate}
\end{figure}

\section{Compensation of the Deterministic Components}
\label{sec:deterministic}

The deterministic components are the systematic and repeatable part of the measurement
errors, such as offsets or trends with the measurement conditions, therefore, if identified they can be compensated . They are identified
on session 07/26\_TUM, and session 08/03\_Kinetiz is then used for validation.
The errors are expressed in the ego frame, as defined
in~\eqref{eq:err_ego}, and the longitudinal component $e_x$ and the lateral component
$e_y$ are analyzed separately.

\subsection{Longitudinal Component}
\label{subsec:det_long}

The analysis of the longitudinal component starts from the two LiDAR pipelines.
Fig.~\ref{fig:det_long} shows their longitudinal error in a window of session
07/26\_TUM: in both cases the error is centered on a constant bias, equal to
\SI{0.40}{m} for the clustering and to \SI{0.36}{m} for PointPillars.  The two pipelines process the same point clouds but
estimate the position of the opponent in different ways: PointPillars regresses the
bounding box of the vehicle, while the clustering compensates the centroid of the
cluster with respect to the corner that is observed. Both, however show the same bias.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch4/det_long_time.pdf}
    \caption[Longitudinal error of the LiDARs]{Longitudinal error of the LiDARs in a window of session 07/26\_TUM, with the mean
    over the session: \SI{0.40}{m} for the clustering and \SI{0.36}{m} for PointPillars.}
    \label{fig:det_long}
\end{figure}

When the analysis is extended to session 08/03\_Kinetiz, the  offset changes: the mean
longitudinal error is \SI{0.02}{m} for the clustering and \SI{0.09}{m} for PointPillars.
The sensors and the detection pipelines are the same in the two
sessions, while the opponent, and therefore the reference, changes. The first hypothesis
is thus that the bias originates from the reference point of the ground truth, which is
assumed to coincide with the point to which the detections are referred
(Section~\ref{sec:gt}): a shift of this point along the longitudinal axis of the opponent
appears as a constant bias of $e_x$.

The analysis is then extended to the longitudinal errors of the camera, shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:det_cam} for session 07/26\_TUM without any compensation, as a function of
the longitudinal position of the opponent. Beyond \SI{60}{m}, the error shows a clear
dependence on the range, so the analysis is restricted to the range 20--\SI{60}{m}. Within
this range, the camera shares the same bias as the LiDARs in both sessions
(Table~\ref{tab:det_long}), with a mean error of \SI{0.35}{m} in session 07/26\_TUM and of
\SI{-0.05}{m} in session 08/03\_Kinetiz; the only difference is a noisier mean, due to a
standard deviation of the error two to four times higher than the one of the LiDARs. The fact that the camera shares the same bias validates the starting hypothesis: the
camera is a different sensor, which relies on a different detection pipeline, so an
error shared with the LiDARs is necessarily related to the reference.

    \caption[Longitudinal error of the camera as a function of $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$]{Longitudinal error of the camera as a function of $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$ in session
    07/26\_TUM, compared with the LiDARs. The shaded area is the operating range.}
    \label{fig:det_cam}
\end{figure}

The longitudinal component of the RaDAR has a specific behavior, which is analyzed in
Section~\ref{sec:macro}.

\subsection{Lateral Component}
\label{subsec:det_lat}

Fig.~\ref{fig:det_lat} shows the lateral error of the LiDARs as a function of the
longitudinal position $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$ of the opponent, for session 07/26\_TUM. The error is
correlated with $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$, and its trend is estimated through a least squares
fit,
\begin{equation}
    e_y = a\,x_{\mathrm{rel}} + b,
    \label{eq:det_lat_fit}
\end{equation}
which gives a slope $a$ of \SI{0.60}{cm/m} for the clustering and \SI{0.58}{cm/m} for
PointPillars. An error proportional to $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$, which changes sign between the
opponent ahead and behind, is the one produced by a small rotation of the measured
positions around the ego vehicle: for a rotation $\delta\psi$, the lateral error is
$e_y \simeq \delta\psi\,x_{\mathrm{rel}}$, and the fitted slope corresponds to
$\delta\psi \simeq \SI{0.3}{\degree}$. Although small, this rotation produces an error that
increases with the range, and reaches about \SI{0.6}{m} at \SI{100}{m}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/ch4/det_lat_xrel.pdf}
    \caption[Lateral error of the LiDARs as a function of $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$]{Lateral error of the LiDARs as a function of $x_{\mathrm{rel}}$ in session 07/26\_TUM,
    with the least squares fit: slope of \SI{0.60}{cm/m} for the clustering and
    \SI{0.58}{cm/m} for PointPillars.}
    \label{fig:det_lat}
\end{figure}

The same slope is found in session 08/03\_Kinetiz, with \SI{0.56}{cm/m} and \SI{0.52}{cm/m}: the
dependence generalizes across the two sessions and is specific of the LiDARs, so it is
related to the ego vehicle and not to the opponent. It is compatible with an offset in
the calibration of the ring on which the three LiDARs are mounted. This dependence is not observed for the
RaDAR, whose slope is close to zero in both sessions (\SI{0.01}{cm/m} and
\SI{0.06}{cm/m}), nor for the camera, whose slope changes from \SI{1.23}{cm/m} in
session 07/26\_TUM to \SI{-0.26}{cm/m} in session 08/03\_Kinetiz and does not generalize.

As a result, the lateral error of the LiDAR detections is compensated by rotating them by
the estimated angle $-\delta\psi$, while the longitudinal offset, being related to the
reference and not to the sensors, is removed from the errors of each session before the
characterization of their random part.
