\chapter{Non-Causal Filtering}
\label{ch:rts}

This chapter presents a non-causal approach to improve the estimate of the opponent's state.
Unlike the classical formulation of the Kalman filter~\cite{kalman1960}, in which the
estimate $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k}$ at instant $k$ is computed only from the measurements
acquired up to that instant, this approach also uses future information, i.e.\ measurements
acquired after instant $k$ up to a later instant $N > k$, to compute the smoothed estimate
$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|N}$. This is achieved through the Rauch--Tung--Striebel (RTS) smoother,
which, for linear Gaussian systems, provides the optimal smoothed state estimate in the
minimum mean square error sense, given all the measurements up to instant
$N$~\cite{rauch1965,sarkka2013book}. By incorporating future measurements into the estimates
of past states, the smoother can mitigate the effects of a mismatch between the filter's
motion model and the actual dynamics of the opponent. During a braking manoeuvre, for
instance, the causal filter may initially continue to predict the opponent's motion
according to the dynamics estimated before braking, detecting the deceleration only as new
measurements arrive. The smoother propagates the information carried by these subsequent
measurements backward in time, correcting the speed and acceleration estimates at instants
when the causal filter had not yet fully captured the change in motion. This can reduce the
lag of the reconstructed braking response and attenuate fluctuations in the estimated state.
The drawback, however, is that the refined estimate refers to a past instant and requires
the acquisition of subsequent measurements before it becomes available. In our application,
this is acceptable because the planning module uses the opponent's state history for gap
control, which regulates the distance from the opponent in terms of time. The smoothed
estimates can therefore refine this history at their corresponding timestamps, while the
most recent state prediction remains causal.

Section~\ref{sec:rts_problem} introduces the smoothing problem and
Section~\ref{sec:rts_formulation} derives the RTS smoother, 
Sections~\ref{sec:rts_implementation} and~\ref{sec:rts_sensitivity} describe its
implementation and present the results. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:rts_qff} proposes its
combination with the feed-forward process noise approach introduced in the previous chapter.

\section{From Filtering to Smoothing}
\label{sec:rts_problem}

Filtering, prediction and smoothing all solve the same problem~\cite{sarkka2013book}, i.e.\ the estimation of the
state $\mathbf{x}_k$ at instant $k$ as the value that maximises its conditional probability
density given a set of measurements $\mathbf{Y}_N = \{\mathbf{z}_1, \dots, \mathbf{z}_N\}$,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|N} = \arg\max_{\mathbf{x}_k}\; p\left(\mathbf{x}_k \mid \mathbf{Y}_N\right),
    \label{eq:rts_map}
\end{equation}
and they differ only in the set of measurements available with respect to the instant of
interest, which determines how the conditional density is computed.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Prediction}, with $N < k$. The measurements are not available up to
    instant $k$, and the density is propagated from the last available one through the
    transition density of the motion model, so that no new information enters the estimate.
    \item \textbf{Filtering}, with $N = k$. The predicted density is complemented with the
    likelihood of the measurement at the current instant,  the Kalman filter
    introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:tracking}.
    \item \textbf{Smoothing}, with $N > k$. Besides the past and current measurements, also those acquired after instant $k$ are used,
and their information corrects the filtered density through the smoothed one at $k+1$.
\end{itemize}

For the smoothing problem, two formulations have been proposed, which have been proven to
be equivalent and, for a linear Gaussian system, to provide the optimal smoothed state~\cite{sarkka2013book}. The
formulation of Fraser and Potter~\cite{fraser1969} proposes the use of two independent Kalman filters running
in parallel, the first evolving forward in time from the first measurement and the second
backward from the last one, so that the smoothed state is obtained as the fusion of their
estimates. The RTS smoother~\cite{rauch1965}, instead,
exploits the recursive structure of the smoothing problem, which links the smoothed density
at $k$ to the one at $k+1$, and computes the smoothed estimate with a backward
recursion that starts from the last instant and runs on the output of a single forward
Kalman filter. The RTS formulation is chosen in this work, since it only requires the
quantities already computed by the forward filter to be stored, and it does not need a
second filter running in parallel with its own initialisation.

\section{RTS Smoother Formulation}
\label{sec:rts_formulation}

The derivation of the closed form of the RTS smoother, introduced by Rauch et al.~\cite{rauch1965}
and following the Bayesian formulation of S\"arkk\"a~\cite{sarkka2013book}, is presented for the linear Gaussian system
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{F}_k\,\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{w}_k,
    \qquad
    \mathbf{z}_k = \mathbf{H}_k\,\mathbf{x}_k + \mathbf{v}_k,
    \label{eq:rts_linear}
\end{equation}
under the following assumptions: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Linearity.} The dynamics and the measurements are linear in the state. For
    the EKF of the tracker, the equations are applied to the system linearised around the
    filtered estimate, with $\mathbf{F}_k$ the Jacobian~\eqref{eq:tt_F} of the motion model.
    \item \textbf{Gaussian white noises.} The process and the measurement noises are
    zero-mean, Gaussian, uncorrelated in time and mutually independent, with covariances
    $\mathbf{Q}_k$ and $\mathbf{R}_k$.
    \item \textbf{Markov property.} The state sequence follows a first-order Markov process:
    given the current state $\mathbf{x}_k$, the next state $\mathbf{x}_{k+1}$ is conditionally
    independent of all previous states,
    $p(\mathbf{x}_{k+1} \mid \mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_k) =
    p(\mathbf{x}_{k+1} \mid \mathbf{x}_k)$.
\end{itemize}

The structure of the smoother is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:rts_scheme}. The forward pass is the
Kalman filter of the tracker, which processes the measurements in chronological order and
stores, at each step, the quantities required by the backward pass. The backward pass is
then initialised at the last step with the filtered estimate and runs in reverse
chronological order, computing each smoothed state from the one of the following step and
from the quantities stored by the forward pass.

\subsection{Forward Pass}
\label{subsec:rts_forward}

 It is the Kalman filter of
Chapter~\ref{ch:tracking} and provides, at each step, the causal quantities on which the
backward recursion operates. The state is first predicted through the motion model,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|k} = \mathbf{F}_k\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k},
    \qquad
    \mathbf{P}_{k+1|k} = \mathbf{F}_k\,\mathbf{P}_{k|k}\,\mathbf{F}_k^\top + \mathbf{Q}_k,
    \label{eq:rts_fwd_pred}
\end{equation}
and then corrected with the measurement,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf{K}_{k+1} &= \mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}\,\mathbf{H}_{k+1}^\top
        \left(\mathbf{H}_{k+1}\,\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}\,\mathbf{H}_{k+1}^\top + \mathbf{R}_{k+1}\right)^{-1}, \\
        \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|k+1} &= \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|k}
        + \mathbf{K}_{k+1}\left(\mathbf{z}_{k+1} - \mathbf{H}_{k+1}\,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|k}\right),
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:rts_fwd_corr}
\end{equation}
as in~\eqref{eq:tt_prediction} and~\eqref{eq:tt_update}. \\ The filtered estimate $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k}$ and its covariance
$\mathbf{P}_{k|k}$ are the causal states that the backward recursion refines, while the
prediction $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|k}$, its covariance $\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}$ and the state
matrix $\mathbf{F}_k$ , as will be shown, are used to compare the causal prediction with the smoothed state at
the following step and to compute the smoother gain, which weights this comparison. 

\subsection{Backward Step}
\label{subsec:rts_backward}

For the backward pass, the main steps of the derivation are presented first, at the end of
which the resulting recursive backward step, implemented in the tracker, is introduced.
Following the principle of maximum likelihood, the smoothed estimates of two consecutive
states, $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|N}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|N}$, are the values that jointly
maximise the logarithm of their conditional density given all the measurements,
\begin{equation}
    L(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{x}_{k+1}) = \log p(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{x}_{k+1} \mid \mathbf{Y}_N),
    \label{eq:rts_L}
\end{equation}
which, since $p(\mathbf{Y}_N)$ does not depend on the states, is equivalent to maximising the
joint density $p(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{x}_{k+1}, \mathbf{Y}_N)$. Splitting the measurements
into those up to instant $k$ and the future ones, and applying the definition of conditional
probability together with the Markov property of the model, the joint density factorises as
\begin{equation}
    p(\mathbf{x}_k, \mathbf{x}_{k+1}, \mathbf{Y}_N) =
    p(\mathbf{z}_{k+1}, \dots, \mathbf{z}_N \mid \mathbf{x}_{k+1})\;
    p(\mathbf{x}_{k+1} \mid \mathbf{x}_k)\;
    p(\mathbf{x}_k \mid \mathbf{Y}_k)\;
    p(\mathbf{Y}_k).
    \label{eq:rts_joint}
\end{equation}
The first factor carries the information of the future measurements and depends only on
$\mathbf{x}_{k+1}$, while the last one does not depend on the states. The second and the
third factors are those provided by the forward pass. In the smoothing problem, the
future state $\mathbf{x}_{k+1}$ is replaced by its smoothed estimate
$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|N}$, which maximises the joint density together with
$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|N}$, and the smoothed estimate at $k$ is obtained by
maximising~\eqref{eq:rts_joint} with respect to $\mathbf{x}_k$ or, equivalently, by
minimising the quadratic cost
\begin{equation}
    J(\mathbf{x}_k) =
    \left\|\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|N} - \mathbf{F}_k\,\mathbf{x}_k\right\|^2_{\mathbf{Q}_k^{-1}}
    + \left\|\mathbf{x}_k - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k}\right\|^2_{\mathbf{P}_{k|k}^{-1}},
    \label{eq:rts_cost}
\end{equation}
whose first term weights the mismatch between the smoothed state at $k+1$ and its prediction
from $\mathbf{x}_k$, and whose second term weights the distance from the filtered estimate.
Setting the gradient of $J$ to zero and applying the matrix inversion lemma, as detailed in
Appendix~\ref{app:rts}, the backward step of the smoother is obtained,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
        \mathbf{C}_k &= \mathbf{P}_{k|k}\,\mathbf{F}_k^\top\,\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}^{-1}, \\
        \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|N} &= \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k}
        + \mathbf{C}_k\left(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|N} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|k}\right), \\
        \mathbf{P}_{k|N} &= \mathbf{P}_{k|k}
        + \mathbf{C}_k\left(\mathbf{P}_{k+1|N} - \mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}\right)\mathbf{C}_k^\top,
    \end{aligned}
    \label{eq:rts_backward}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{C}_k$ is the smoother gain and the covariance follows from the propagation of
the estimation errors through the update of the state. At the last instant, the measurements
used by the filter and by the smoother coincide, so that $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{N|N}$ and
$\mathbf{P}_{N|N}$ are also the smoothed estimates, and~\eqref{eq:rts_backward} is applied
backward for $k = N-1, \dots, 0$, using at each step the smoothed estimate computed at the
previous one and the quantities stored by the forward pass.

The correction applied by the smoother to the causal state is determined by two factors.
The first is the mismatch $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|N} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|k}$ between the
smoothed state and the causal prediction at $k+1$, which quantifies the information
contributed by the future measurements beyond what the motion model can predict. It remains
small as long as the model describes the dynamics of the opponent correctly, and it grows when
the dynamics changes, e.g.\ at the beginning of a manoeuvre, when the subsequent measurements
reveal an evolution that the prediction could not anticipate. The second is the gain
$\mathbf{C}_k = \mathbf{P}_{k|k}\mathbf{F}_k^\top\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}^{-1}$, which weights this
mismatch according to the uncertainty of the forward filter, i.e.\ to the ratio between the
covariance of the filtered estimate and the one of the prediction. A filtered estimate with a
small covariance is therefore only marginally corrected, whereas an uncertain one is pulled
more strongly towards the information carried by the future estimates. The same
mechanism acts on the covariance.  The term
$\mathbf{C}_k(\mathbf{P}_{k+1|N} - \mathbf{P}_{k+1|k})\mathbf{C}_k^\top$
in~\eqref{eq:rts_backward} is negative semidefinite, and by induction backward in time
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{P}_{k|N} \preceq \mathbf{P}_{k|k}, \qquad k = 0, \dots, N,
    \label{eq:rts_cov_bound}
\end{equation}
so that the smoothed estimate is never more uncertain than the filtered one, and its
uncertainty is reduced more where the forward filter is less confident and the future
measurements bring more information.

Finally, recalling from Section~\ref{sec:rts_problem} that filtering and smoothing solve the
same estimation problem, it can be noted that the backward step has the same structure as the
correction step of the Kalman filter, as summarised in Table~\ref{tab:kf_rts}. In the filter, the gain $\mathbf{K}_{k+1}$
corrects the predicted state with the measurement $\mathbf{z}_{k+1}$, related to the state
through $\mathbf{H}_{k+1}$ with an uncertainty $\mathbf{R}_{k+1}$. In the smoother, the gain
$\mathbf{C}_k$ corrects the filtered state with the smoothed state at $k+1$, which acts as a
pseudo-measurement related to $\mathbf{x}_k$ through the motion model $\mathbf{F}_k$ with an
uncertainty $\mathbf{Q}_k$, and in both cases the same gain also corrects the covariance of
the estimate.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:rts_implementation}
\label{subsec:rts_window}

Depending on which states are refined and on when the smoothed estimate is computed, three
classes of smoothers are distinguished. A fixed-point smoother refines the estimate of a
single instant of interest as new measurements arrive. A fixed-interval smoother computes
the estimates of all the states of an interval once all its measurements have been acquired,
which is the problem solved by the formulation of Section~\ref{sec:rts_formulation}. A
fixed-lag smoother, finally, provides at each instant the estimate of the state at a fixed
delay behind the current time, i.e.\ $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k-L|k}$ with a constant lag $L$. In
the tracker, the estimate has to be
available at every publication, so that a fixed-lag formulation is adopted, obtained by
applying the fixed-interval RTS recursion on a window that slides with the current time.

The smoother is implemented on a sliding window of fixed duration $T_w$, so that, from the
current instant $t_N$, i.e.\ the time of the most recent entry of the history of the tracker,
it smooths backward in time over the interval $[t_N - T_w,\, t_N]$, using all the measurements
contained in it. This choice is preferred to a formulation that fixes the information used by
the smoother, i.e.\ the number of measurements, for two reasons. First, the time span covered
by a fixed number of measurements would change with the number of the detections,
being short when the opponent is observed by all the sensors and longer when only some of them
are available. Second, the estimate is used by the gap control, which regulates the distance
from the opponent in terms of time, and, since the smoother replaces a real-time estimate with
one referred to the past, it is preferable to bound how old the smoothed information can be
rather than how much information is used to compute it.

At every publication, the backward step of Section~\ref{subsec:rts_backward} is applied over
the window, as reported in Algorithm~\ref{alg:rts}. The recursion is initialised at the most recent entry and proceeds backward to the oldest one
of the window, computing at each step the smoothed state from the causal one and from the
result of the previous step. In the end, the history used by the planning module contains the causal
prediction of the filter at the most recent instant and, from the first available measurement
backward, the smoothed states.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{RTS smoother on the sliding window of the history, for a single track.}
\label{alg:rts}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE entries $k = 0, \dots, N$ of the history of the track in the window
$[t_N - T_w,\, t_N]$, storing $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k}$, $\mathbf{P}_{k|k}$,
$\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|k}$, $\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}$ and $\mathbf{F}_k$
\ENSURE smoothed estimates $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|N}$ and $\mathbf{P}_{k|N}$
\IF{the smoother is disabled \OR $N < 1$}
    \RETURN the causal estimates
\ENDIF
\STATE $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{N|N},\, \mathbf{P}_{N|N} \leftarrow$ filtered estimate \COMMENT{initialisation}
\FOR{$k = N-1$ \textbf{down to} $0$}
    \IF{the prediction $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|k}$, $\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}$ is stored}
        \STATE $\mathbf{C}_k \leftarrow \mathbf{P}_{k|k}\,\mathbf{F}_k^\top\,\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}^{-1}$
        \STATE $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|N} \leftarrow \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|k} + \mathbf{C}_k\left(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|N} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|k}\right)$
        \STATE $\mathbf{P}_{k|N} \leftarrow \mathbf{P}_{k|k} + \mathbf{C}_k\left(\mathbf{P}_{k+1|N} - \mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}\right)\mathbf{C}_k^\top$
    \ELSE
        \STATE $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|N},\, \mathbf{P}_{k|N} \leftarrow$ filtered estimate \COMMENT{causal fallback}
    \ENDIF
\ENDFOR
\RETURN $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|N}$, $\mathbf{P}_{k|N}$ for $k = 0, \dots, N$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Degenerate cases, such as a track that has just been initialised or a state without future
support, fall back to the causal estimate, so that the output is always complete. Moreover,
since the smoother only writes on the published history and leaves the estimates of the
forward filter unchanged, it is also robust to the out-of-sequence measurements
(Section~\ref{subsec:tt_oosm}). When a late measurement arrives, the forward filter inserts
it according to its acquisition time and reprocesses the following entries, updating the
stored filtered states, predictions and Jacobians, so that the measurement is automatically
included in the next backward pass without any smoothed state having to be corrected.

\section{Sensitivity Analysis and Results}
\label{sec:rts_sensitivity}

Since the smoother maximises the density of the state conditioned on the measurements
$\mathbf{Y}_N$, the parameter that most influences the quality of the smoothing is $N$, i.e.\
the number of measurements acquired after the instant of interest, which in our
implementation is set by the window length $T_w$. In the following, a sensitivity analysis
on the window length is therefore carried out, to quantify its effect on the smoothed
estimate and to identify its optimal value. The analysis is performed on the speed of the
opponent, compared with the ground truth through two metrics.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RMSE of the speed}. Denoting by $\hat{v}_i(T_w)$ the speed estimated at
    the instant $t_i$ with a window $T_w$, i.e.\ smoothed with the measurements acquired up
    to $t_i + T_w$, and by $v_i$ the speed of the ground truth interpolated at the same
    instant, the error is summarised as
    \begin{equation}
        \mathrm{RMSE}(T_w) = \sqrt{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(\hat{v}_i(T_w) - v_i\right)^2},
        \label{eq:rts_rmse}
    \end{equation}
    The RMSE is computed both on the whole session (global RMSE) and on the
    instants within the braking manoeuvres (local RMSE).

    \item \textbf{Manoeuvre delay}. Evaluates whether the smoother, by exploiting
    the future information, reduces the delay with which the estimate detects the onset of a
    manoeuvre. The braking manoeuvres are identified on the ground truth of the opponent; the same instant is then searched on the speed estimated by the smoother with different
    window lengths, and the manoeuvre delay is the difference between the two. In our results, a  positive value
    indicates that the estimate starts the manoeuvre late, a negative one that it anticipates
    it, the delay is summarised by its median over the braking manoeuvres of the session.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Sensitivity Analysis with All Sensors Available}

First, the performance of the smoother is analysed when all the sensors are available, i.e.\
with the position measurements of the LiDARs, of the camera and of the RaDAR, and with the
range rate measurement of the RaDAR. Fig.~\ref{fig:rts_lag_radar} shows the performance of
the smoother on the estimate of the speed of the opponent, comparing the one-step-ahead
prediction of the filter, the causal state, and the
smoothed states obtained with an increasing amount of future information, up to a window of
\SI{500}{ms}. As the window increases, the smoothed state corrects to a greater extent the
noise and the spurious peaks of the causal estimate, although the smoothed states tend to
overlap for windows of about 200 to \SI{300}{ms}.

Looking at the RMSE, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:rts_metrics_radar} and reported in
Table~\ref{tab:rts_radar} as a function of the window length together with the manoeuvre delay,
it can be seen that the causal state, which is the baseline of the analysis, is more accurate
than the one-step-ahead prediction, since it
integrates the latest measurements, including the range rate of the RaDAR, with a global RMSE
of \SI{0.938}{m/s} against \SI{1.177}{m/s}. With respect to this baseline, the smoother reduces
the global and the local RMSE by \SI{31}{\percent} and \SI{35}{\percent}, respectively. The
same overlap observed in Fig.~\ref{fig:rts_lag_radar} is evident here, since the reduction of
the RMSE grows with the window up to about \SI{300}{ms} and then saturates.

This saturation follows from how the information of the future measurements reaches the
state at instant $k$. From the backward step~\eqref{eq:rts_backward}, a variation of the
smoothed state at $k+1$ is transferred to the one at $k$ through the gain,
$\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|N}/\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+1|N} = \mathbf{C}_k$. Applying
the step recursively, a variation of the state at $k+m$, such as the one produced by a new
measurement, reaches the state at $k$ through the product of the gains,
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k|N}}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{k+m|N}}
    = \mathbf{C}_k\mathbf{C}_{k+1}\cdots\mathbf{C}_{k+m-1}.
    \label{eq:rts_gain_product}
\end{equation}
Each gain $\mathbf{C}_k = \mathbf{P}_{k|k}\mathbf{F}_k^\top\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}^{-1}$ compares
the filtered covariance $\mathbf{P}_{k|k}$ with the predicted one $\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}$. With
the range rate of the RaDAR, the speed is measured directly and enters every correction step,
lowering its variance in $\mathbf{P}_{k|k}$, which is therefore much smaller than in
$\mathbf{P}_{k+1|k}$. The gain on this component is therefore small, and the
product~\eqref{eq:rts_gain_product} decreases rapidly, vanishing within about \SI{300}{ms}. Measurements acquired beyond this horizon no
longer reach the state at $k$, so widening the window brings no further reduction of the RMSE.

\subsection*{Sensitivity Analysis without Speed Measurements}
\label{subsec:rts_radar}

Given how the RTS incorporates future information into the estimate it corrects, it is also
of interest to investigate the case in which only position measurements are available, i.e.\
without the RaDAR. This is the worst case for the estimation of the derived dynamics, since
speed and acceleration are no longer measured and must be reconstructed by the forward filter
as the first and second time derivatives of the position provided by the LiDARs and the
camera. The forward filter is therefore expected to be more uncertain on these components.
Based on~\eqref{eq:rts_backward} and~\eqref{eq:rts_gain_product}, the hypothesis is that the
larger entries of $\mathbf{P}_{k|k}$ increase the gain $\mathbf{C}_k$, so that future
information is weighted more and, through the product of the gains, propagated further back
in time. The analysis is carried out to verify this
hypothesis and to quantify whether, and to what extent, the use of future estimates is
beneficial in this configuration.

Fig.~\ref{fig:rts_lag_noradar} shows that, in this case, the one-step-ahead prediction and
the causal state lag behind the ground truth more markedly than in the previous case, and
that the RTS estimates obtained with the different windows are clearly separated, i.e.\ the
correction applied by the RTS to the causal state is considerably larger.

The results are reported in Fig.~\ref{fig:rts_metrics_noradar} and in
Table~\ref{tab:rts_noradar}. The one-step-ahead prediction has a global RMSE of
\SI{2.056}{m/s}, which is reduced to \SI{1.533}{m/s} by the causal state, again the baseline
of the analysis. Without the range rate, the filtered variance of the speed is larger, the
gain $\mathbf{C}_k$ increases and the future measurements are propagated further back in
time, so that the reduction of the RMSE reaches \SI{73}{\percent} and saturates only at about
\SI{600}{ms}.

Table~\ref{tab:rts_noradar} also shows that the smoother corrects the delay of the
one-step-ahead prediction and of the causal state, which start the manoeuvres about
\SI{290}{ms} and \SI{190}{ms} after the ground truth, but, as the window widens, it exhibits a
behaviour that leads it to anticipate the onset of the manoeuvre, with an anticipation that grows
up to about \SI{500}{ms}, where it saturates at about \SI{-106}{ms}. The same behaviour is visible in the
local RMSE, computed on the braking manoeuvres, which reaches its minimum of \SI{0.669}{m/s} at
about \SI{500}{ms} and then increases, up to \SI{0.820}{m/s} at \SI{981}{ms}.

This behaviour is explained by~\eqref{eq:rts_gain_product}. Without the measurement of the
range rate of the RaDAR, the filtered covariance $\mathbf{P}_{k|k}$ is no longer corrected by
a direct observation of the speed, and the forward filter remains more uncertain on the speed and
the acceleration. The gains $\mathbf{C}_k$ are therefore larger, their product decays more
slowly and, as a consequence, the effect of the measurements is propagated further back in
time. During braking, this backward propagation carries information about the deceleration to
states preceding the actual onset of the manoeuvre. The corresponding speed estimates are
therefore corrected downwards, causing the reconstructed braking response to begin earlier
than in the ground truth.

\section{Combination with the Q Feed-Forward}
\label{sec:rts_qff}

As a final step, the RTS smoother is combined with the feed-forward process noise approach introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:qff}. As discussed at the end of that chapter, although the feed-forward strategy improves the responsiveness of the filter during manoeuvres, its effectiveness is limited by the quality of the jerk estimate used for detection. In particular, the delay inherited from the causal acceleration estimate and the noise introduced by differentiation can lead to late or spurious activations of the manoeuvre flag.

The RTS smoother provides a complementary way to address these limitations. By exploiting measurements acquired after the instant being estimated, it can compensate for the lag accumulated by the causal filter before the manoeuvre is detected. At the same time, the backward recursion attenuates the fluctuations and possible overshoots introduced by the increased process noise, including those associated with spurious activations. Lower manoeuvre detection thresholds than those selected in Chapter~\ref{ch:qff} can therefore be used, enabling earlier detection and process noise inflation, and providing the smoother with a causal estimate that better represents the vehicle's dynamics.

Their combination is also straightforward from an implementation perspective. As described in Section~\ref{sec:rts_implementation}, the smoother operates on the history of the causal filter, which already contains the estimates obtained with the feed-forward process noise. The two algorithms can therefore be combined without modifying either the manoeuvre detection logic or the backward recursion.

The combined approach is evaluated using the smoothing window of \SI{300}{ms} and the process noise inflation factor $\lambda = 100$ selected in the previous chapters, while, for the reasons discussed above, a more aggressive tuning of the manoeuvre detection is adopted, with $j_{\mathrm{on}} = \SI{7}{m/s^3}$ and $j_{\mathrm{off}} = \SI{3}{m/s^3}$. Figs.~\ref{fig:rts_qff_2} and~\ref{fig:rts_qff_4} compare three estimates over two sequences of corners: the causal estimate with feed-forward process noise, with the nominal tuning of Chapter~\ref{ch:qff}, the RTS smoother applied to the nominal filter, with the nominal window selected in Section~\ref{sec:rts_sensitivity}, and the RTS smoother combined with the feed-forward process noise, with the tuning described above.

The figures show how the smoother mitigates the limitations of the feed-forward strategy. The late activations of the manoeuvre flag, observed for instance around \SI{1064}{s}, \SI{1078}{s} and \SI{1086}{s} in Fig.~\ref{fig:rts_qff_4}, no longer result in a delayed onset of the smoothed braking response, which is closely aligned with the ground truth. Similarly, the spikes caused by spurious activations, such as those around \SI{1066}{s} and \SI{1068}{s}, and the overshoots associated with the increased process noise are considerably attenuated.

The comparison between the two smoothed estimates also highlights the benefit of combining the approaches. Although the RTS smoother alone improves the nominal estimate, a residual delay remains during the braking phases. When combined with the feed-forward, instead, the backward recursion operates on a causal estimate that already responds more rapidly to changes in the opponent's dynamics. The subsequent information can therefore refine a more representative estimate, further reducing the lag and bringing the reconstructed speed and acceleration closer to the ground truth.

These improvements are quantified in Table~\ref{tab:rts_qff}, which reports the RMSE of the speed and acceleration with respect to the ground truth. The causal feed-forward estimate is used as the reference, since it represents the output available from the filter in real time. Both smoothed estimates reduce the acceleration RMSE by approximately \SI{50}{\percent}, reaching \SI{1.869}{m/s^2} with the nominal smoother and \SI{1.786}{m/s^2} with the combined approach. The difference is more pronounced for the speed: the nominal smoother reduces the RMSE by \SI{40.7}{\percent}, from \SI{1.069}{m/s} to \SI{0.634}{m/s}, whereas the combination with the feed-forward achieves a reduction of \SI{66.7}{\percent}, reaching \SI{0.356}{m/s}.
